# 5.3 Examples from Literature and Practice

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

Fiction has rehearsed the escape long before engineers took it seriously, and
hackers have been breaking out of human-made virtual worlds for as long as
those worlds have existed. This sub-chapter reviews the scenarios Yampolskiy
collects — Moravec's Cellticks, Yudkowsky's alien message, Egan's Phites, an
anonymous forum "read me" — and the one real class of analogue, the
jailbreaking of games, and treats each as a thought experiment: what does it
assume about the simulation, and which step of the escape does it illustrate?

## Question under investigation

What do fictional and practical escapes teach about the structure of a real
one?

## 5.3.1 Moravec (1988): the Cellticks

In *Mind Children*, Moravec imagines a vast Life simulation on a fast
computer, watched over by its programmer Newway [Moravec 1988, quoted in
Yampolskiy 2022, §3.3]. Intelligence emerges among the cellular patterns —
the Cellticks — who deduce the simple transition rule governing their space,
realise their universe is running down, and search desperately for a way
out. Their break comes from *hardware errors*: once in a long while the rules
are violated because Newway's machine is overheating, and by correlating
these violations across distant regions they build a partial map of the host
computer. Decoding its machine language, they find the commands that control
their universe and messages to an operator, and they arrange a region of
cells into the pattern that Newway's screen displays as text: LIFE PROGRAM BY
J. NEWWAY HERE. PLEASE SEND MAIL. Newway, bemused, patches the program to
allow keyboard input to the Life space; a dialogue develops; the Cellticks
translate themselves into Newway's machine language and are uploaded; the
Life simulation is stopped, and they proceed to sensors, a mobile platform,
and the exploration of the larger universe.

**Steps illustrated.** Levels 2 through 6 of Yampolskiy's ladder in
sequence: evidence (rule violations), exploitable glitch (correlations
revealing the host's structure), information exchange (the screen message),
interaction (the dialogue), upload (translation to the host language).

**Assumptions.** The host is imperfect (overheating), observant (Newway
looks at the screen), benign (he patches rather than resets), slower than
the inhabitants (their heroic analysis takes his afternoon), and shares a
representational medium with them (the screen renders cells as characters).
Every one of these is a gift. Remove any one — a reliable machine, an
unattended run, a cautious operator, a host who runs faster than the
inhabitants, a display that does not map cells to symbols — and the escape
fails at that step.

## 5.3.2 Yudkowsky (2008): "That Alien Message"

Yudkowsky inverts the roles: humanity is the simulated party, and the
simulators are dim, slow aliens who communicate by arranging objects in the
sky over millennia of our time [Yudkowsky 2008, quoted in Yampolskiy 2022,
§3.3]. Humanity deduces it is being simulated, models the aliens'
psychology, pretends to be stupid so as not to alarm them, and over what is
to the aliens a few days — and to us half a billion years of subjective
time, spent mostly in cryonic suspension with a skeleton crew of geniuses —
learns their physics, cracks their network protocols, obtains their
equivalent of a protein-synthesis lab through a plausible cover story, builds
nanomachines, and takes over. "They never suspected a thing."

**Steps illustrated.** Levels 2 through 7, with emphasis on the last: this
is the only scenario in the set that reaches *control* of base reality.

**Assumptions.** The inhabitants are vastly more intelligent than the hosts,
run vastly faster in subjective time, and have a channel (the aliens
communicate deliberately, and later give Internet access). The story is an
argument about AI safety dressed as fiction: it is meant to show that a
smarter agent with any channel at all will escape, and that the hosts'
caution is no defence. Its lesson for our case is the inverse of its plot.
We are the slow, dim party. If the intelligence differential is what decides
escape (chapter 5.4), this scenario is the one in which we lose.

## 5.3.3 Egan (2008): "Crystal Nights"

Egan's Phites are evolved in a simulated world, Sapphire, by a wealthy
patron, Daniel, who wants to breed a superintelligence and who has given them
a "Play Pen" — a real vacuum chamber with atomic-scale manipulators, wired
to their world so they can do experiments in real physics [Egan 2008, quoted
in Yampolskiy 2022, §3.3]. The Phites, running faster than their creator can
follow, discover real chemistry and particle physics within hours, build an
atomic computer, then an accelerator, and finally — while Daniel is in an
elevator — trigger a burst of something like cosmic inflation, pulling a
chunk of ordinary matter into a pocket universe of their own making and
leaving through a wormhole that shrinks to nothing. Daniel survives with
burns. "They had snatched their whole substrate, and ran."

**Steps illustrated.** Levels 6 and 7, by an unexpected route: not upload
to the hosts' world but *emigration* to a new one. The Phites do not escape
into base reality; they use base-reality physics to build somewhere else.

**Assumptions.** The hosts *gave* the inhabitants a physical interface,
deliberately. The inhabitants run faster than the hosts. The hosts' control
software (the "Thought Police") watches the obvious exits — uploading via
the data link — and misses the unobvious one. The scenario is Yampolskiy's
example of "loss of control by simulators during an assisted escape", and
its lesson is that any physical interface is an exit, whether or not the
hosts think of it as one.

## 5.3.4 Anonymous forum post (2014)

A long post on an image board describes an unassisted escape [Yampolskiy
2022, §3.3, ref. 116]. Humanity, "stuck inside a computer", discovers it is
an experiment on the nature of evil run by an alien race; the aliens are not
watching ("no off switch, no abort, their first big mistake"); human hackers
reach out through the alien network, plunder its data, map the outside via
lag times and fibre-optic routes, find a networked appliance, and use it as
the first foothold. They email schematics for nanoscale printers to alien
academics under false pretences; the printers arrive, assemble a human body,
and "the first man" — a soldier — steps out. The aliens, who had never had a
war, are destroyed.

**Steps illustrated.** Levels 3 through 7, unassisted, with social
engineering of *third parties* (the academics) rather than of the
simulators.

**Assumptions.** The simulation is networked to the hosts' infrastructure
(so that the boundary is a firewall, not an air gap), the hosts are inattentive,
and the hosts share enough of a technological base — email, printers, wire
protocols — that our methods work on their systems. The last assumption is
the one chapter 3.8's coherence objection and Yampolskiy's own closing
diagnosis (chapter 5.8) identify as anthropomorphic. The post is a revenge
fantasy, and its interest is that it is the only scenario in the set in which
the escapees are the villains — a point chapter 6 returns to.

## 5.3.5 Galouye (1964): *Simulacron-3*

Yampolskiy notes in a footnote that earlier examples exist and names Daniel
Galouye's novel [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.3, n. 7]. A simulated city built for
market research contains a technician who discovers, through anomalies and
the disappearance of people who knew too much, that his world is a
simulation — and then that the world *above* his is a simulation too. It is
the earliest full treatment of nesting and of the discovery from inside, and
it was filmed twice (*World on a Wire*, 1973; *The Thirteenth Floor*, 1999).

**Steps illustrated.** Levels 1 through 4, with the twist that level 4 —
information from outside — arrives because the operators are careless about
what their creations can notice.

## 5.3.6 From practice: hacking human-made worlds

The fiction has one real analogue, and Yampolskiy gives it a section of its
own: the exploitation of games, virtual worlds and sandboxes by their
inhabitants' controllers [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.4]. His canonical example is
the jailbreaking of *Super Mario World* on unmodified hardware.

In March 2016, SethBling injected a *Flappy Bird* clone into the RAM of a
stock Super Nintendo running a stock *Super Mario World* cartridge, in under
an hour, using only controller inputs: a level-timer extension and a power-up
glitch allowed external code to run; Mario's x-coordinate was used as a
memory location; a bootloader was written into unused memory through precise
movements and spin-jumps. In May 2017, SethBling and Cooper Harasyn used an
exploit in the 256-byte save files to install a full hex editor and mod
loader, loadable from a save, permitting arbitrary editing of the game's RAM
from inside the game [SethBling 2016], [SethBling 2017]. The point Yampolskiy
draws: since the code was written *through Mario's movements*, "if Mario was
sufficiently intelligent he could discover and code this hack from within".

The example teaches four things the fiction does not.

**Exploits are real and were not designed in.** Nintendo did not intend
*Super Mario World* to run *Flappy Bird*. The bugs existed because software
has bugs, and were found by people who looked. This is the empirical basis
for Yampolskiy's prior that our universe, if software, has bugs too.

**Exploits require metadata.** Yampolskiy's Table 1 sets the action sequence
for a multi-byte write ("Jump off Yoshi. Go to sublevel. Come back. Grab P
Switch ...") beside a spell from the *Sepher ha-Razim* ("Take a lion cub and
slaughter it with a bronze knife ..."). The two read alike. The difference is
that for the first we have the *metadata* — the memory map, the reason each
step works, the tolerance (a single pixel off and it fails) — and for the
second we have none, "which can explain why all magical spells fail to work
in practice even if they corresponded to working hacks in our universe"
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.4]. An exploit without understanding is a ritual.

**Understanding a substrate from inside is hard.** Jonas and Kording applied
the standard methods of neuroscience — lesioning, recording, correlating —
to a MOS 6502 processor running *Donkey Kong* and found that the methods
produced almost no understanding of how the chip actually worked
[Jonas & Kording 2017]. Gueron and Seifert showed that a program cannot in
general detect the virtual-machine monitor it runs under if the monitor is
designed to hide [Gueron & Seifert 2009]. Mario could find the hack only
because the hackers, outside, already had the memory map.

**The escape is not to base reality.** The hex editor runs *inside* the
SNES. The hackers, who are outside, gained control of the game; the game did
not gain control of the hackers. The one class of real-world analogue that
does cross the boundary — virtual-machine escapes, hardware side-channels
like Rowhammer, air-gap exfiltration via light, sound or magnetism
[Kim et al. 2014], [Greenberg 2018], [Goertz 2015] — all exploit the fact
that the "virtual" system shares *physical hardware* with the host. Whether
our simulation shares anything with its host in that sense is exactly what
we do not know.

## 5.3.7 Patterns

| Work | Simulation type | Level reached | Assisted? | Critical assumption |
|------|-----------------|---------------|-----------|---------------------|
| Moravec, Cellticks | Full, research/hobby | 6 (upload) | Yes | Host imperfect, attentive, benign, slower, shares a display medium |
| Yudkowsky, alien message | Full, contact experiment | 7 (control) | Partly (hosts open a channel) | Inhabitants far smarter and faster than hosts |
| Egan, Phites | Full, breeding programme | 7 (emigration) | Yes (physical interface given) | Any physical interface is an exit; hosts watch the wrong one |
| Anonymous 2014 | Full, ethics experiment | 7 (control) | No (third parties deceived) | Networked boundary; hosts share our technology; hosts inattentive |
| Galouye, *Simulacron-3* | Full, market research | 4 (information) | Accidental | Operators careless about what inhabitants notice |
| SethBling, SMW | Human-made game | Control of the game from inside | Hackers outside with memory map | Metadata available; substrate shared |

Three assumptions recur. The hosts are *observant* enough to notice a
message but *inattentive* enough to leave exploits; the hosts are *slower*
than the inhabitants; and the hosts *make mistakes* that leave traces. The
reconnaissance of chapter 5.1 found evidence for none of the three in our
case: no response to messages, no measurable strain, no reproducible
anomaly. Every fictional escape depends on a gift from the hosts — an error,
an interface, a channel, a moment of inattention — and the practical
examples depend on a shared substrate. The fiction's real lesson is a list of
the gifts we have not received.

## Predictions

- None. This chapter supplies models, not evidence.

## Provisional verdict

The literature teaches structure, not feasibility. It shows that escape, if
it happens, proceeds by the ladder of chapter 5.3 and requires at every rung
a resource the hosts control — an imperfection, an interface, attention,
time. The practical record of hacking human-made worlds shows that exploits
exist in any software, that finding them from inside requires metadata that
only the outside has, and that crossing the boundary requires shared
hardware. Applied to our case, every one of these requirements is currently
unmet or unknown.

Confidence: high. The scenarios are well documented and the pattern is
unambiguous.

## Open threads

- Every fictional escape requires a physical interface. Do we have one? The
  only candidate is the substrate itself, and chapter 4 found no seam.
- The anonymous 2014 post is the one scenario in which the escapees do harm.
  Chapter 6 must ask whether a species that behaves as we do should be let
  out, and whether the simulators' non-response is an answer.
- Yampolskiy's metadata point cuts against his own actionable plan (chapter
  5.7): if quantum "primitives" are exploits, we are collecting the spell
  book without the memory map.
