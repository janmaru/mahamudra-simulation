# 2.1 Clues from Physics

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

If the universe is computed, it might carry the fingerprints of computation:
a smallest length, a fastest speed, values stored only when read, resource
optimisation, or outright glitches. This sub-chapter examines each candidate
fingerprint, states what mainstream physics says about it, and asks whether
the simulation reading adds anything.

## Question under investigation

Does any known feature of physics look more like a computational artefact than
like a brute fact?

## The test applied to each clue

Every item below is put through the same three questions.

1. **Is X true?** Is the feature actually established, or is it a popular
   gloss on something more complicated?
2. **Does standard physics produce X?** If a well-tested theory already
   predicts the feature, the simulation reading is redundant unless it
   predicts something *further*.
3. **Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** If it does, that
   further prediction is what chapter 4 should test. If it does not, the
   item stays at grade B.

## 2.1.1 Discreteness

**The clue.** Physics has natural units — the Planck length, about
1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m, and the Planck time, about 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s — below which our
theories stop making sense. A computer simulating space would use a grid, and
a grid has a spacing. Perhaps the Planck scale is the spacing.

**Is it true?** Only partly. The Planck scale is where quantum mechanics and
general relativity are both expected to matter, and where our current theories
lose validity. It is not established that space *is* discrete at that scale.
Some approaches to quantum gravity (loop quantum gravity, causal sets) do
quantise spacetime; others (string theory) do not in the same sense. The
Planck length is a limit of knowledge, not a measured pixel.

**Does standard physics produce it?** The Planck scale is derived from three
constants — *c*, *G* and *ħ* — by dimensional analysis. Its existence needs no
grid. Furthermore, a fixed spatial grid would break Lorentz symmetry: it would
define a preferred frame and preferred directions. Lorentz symmetry has been
tested to extraordinary precision. The Fermi satellite's observation of a
31 GeV photon from gamma-ray burst 090510, arriving within a second of
lower-energy photons after a journey of seven billion years, constrains any
linear-order energy dependence of the speed of light to an energy scale above
the Planck mass [Abdo et al. 2009]; later analysis pushed the bound to more
than seven times the Planck energy [Vasileiou et al. 2013]. A naive
Planck-scale grid would violate these bounds.

**Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** Yes, and this is the
one place in the physics case where a prediction has been worked out. Beane,
Davoudi and Savage asked what signature a universe simulated on a cubic
lattice, in the manner of lattice QCD, would leave [Beane, Davoudi & Savage
2012]. Their answer: the lattice would impose a maximum energy on particles
(the inverse of the spacing), and it would break rotational symmetry, so the
highest-energy cosmic rays should arrive preferentially along the lattice
axes. They noted that the observed cutoff in the cosmic-ray spectrum at around
5 × 10¹⁹ eV — the Greisen–Zatsepin–Kuzmin (GZK) cutoff — could in principle be
a lattice artefact if the spacing were around 10⁻²⁷ m.

**Status of the observation.** The GZK cutoff is observed, by the Pierre Auger
Observatory and the Telescope Array. It is also predicted by ordinary physics:
protons above that energy lose energy by scattering off the cosmic microwave
background, so the cutoff is expected without any lattice. Beane and
colleagues' own estimate is that the lattice spacing would have to be at or
below about 10⁻¹² fm for the GZK mechanism not to hide it
[Beane, Davoudi & Savage 2012].

The cubic anisotropy is a different matter, and here the record must be
stated carefully. Auger reported in 2017 a large-scale *dipole* anisotropy
above 8 × 10¹⁸ eV, with an amplitude of about 6.5 % and a direction that
points away from the Galactic centre, indicating an extragalactic origin
[Auger 2017]. A dipole is not the cubic pattern a lattice would impose, and
its presence shows that the sky at these energies is dominated by the
distribution of sources. But **no dedicated search for cubic symmetry in
arrival directions has been published** by Auger, the Telescope Array, or
their joint working group. The prediction was made in 2012 and, as far as
this book can determine, has never been directly tested. It is constrained
indirectly — a lattice coarse enough to produce a visible cubic pattern would
also violate the Lorentz-invariance bounds above — but "indirectly
constrained" is not "tested and failed", and an earlier draft of this chapter
overstated the case.

**Grade.** B. The one worked-out physical prediction in the entire simulation
literature is untested. That is a gap in the observational record rather than
a result, and chapter 4 lists closing it as cheap and worthwhile. Even a
negative result, when it comes, would not rule out a finer lattice, a
non-cubic one, or a simulation with no lattice at all — but "consistent with
a lattice too fine to see" is not evidence for a lattice.

## 2.1.2 A maximum speed

**The clue.** Nothing carries information faster than light. In a cellular
automaton, nothing propagates faster than one cell per tick. Perhaps *c* is
the tick rate.

**Is it true?** Yes. The invariance of *c* is among the best-tested facts in
physics.

**Does standard physics produce it?** Yes. In special relativity the speed of
light is not a speed limit imposed on an otherwise Newtonian world; it is a
consequence of the geometry of spacetime, in which space and time are
components of a single structure and *c* is the conversion factor between
them. Every massless particle moves at *c*; nothing with mass reaches it. The
structure is Lorentz-invariant: there is no frame in which the "ticks" happen.

**Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** It predicts the
opposite of what is observed. A cellular automaton with a tick has a preferred
frame — the one in which the grid is at rest — and in that frame the maximum
speed is exactly one cell per tick, while in others it is direction-dependent.
Relativity has no such frame. (The cosmic microwave background does define a
frame in which the universe's matter is on average at rest, but that is a
frame of the *contents*, not of the *laws*; the laws look identical in every
frame.) A simulator who wanted to reproduce Lorentz invariance on a grid would
have to work hard to hide the grid, and would have no reason to make *c* the
tick rate rather than any other value.

**Grade.** B, and the weakest of the set. The analogy is superficial: the
thing a speed limit would look like in a simulation is not what *c* looks
like.

## 2.1.3 Quantum indeterminacy and measurement

**The clue.** A quantum system does not have a definite position, or spin,
until it is measured. A program written for efficiency does not compute a
value until it is needed. Perhaps measurement is when the universe calls the
function.

**Is it true?** The physics is true; the gloss is not. Quantum systems do
lack definite values for some observables before measurement, and this is not
ignorance of a hidden value — Bell-test experiments have ruled out the simple
hidden-variable alternatives. But "measurement" in quantum mechanics does not
mean observation by a conscious being. It means any interaction that
entangles the system with a large environment. A photon striking a dust grain
is a measurement. The universe was "measuring" itself for billions of years
before there was anyone to look.

**Does standard physics produce it?** The theory of decoherence explains why
interaction with an environment makes superpositions unobservable in practice,
without any reference to observers, and does so quantitatively. What remains
unsettled is the interpretation — whether collapse is a real physical process,
an artefact of our description, or a branching — but every interpretation
agrees on the predictions, and none of them requires an observer in the sense
the simulation reading needs.

**Delayed choice.** The delayed-choice quantum eraser is often cited as
showing that the universe "waits to see" whether it will be observed before
deciding how to behave. The experiment is real and its results are exactly as
quantum mechanics predicts. The retrocausal gloss comes from describing the
results in a particular order; the standard analysis shows that no
information travels backwards, and the pattern only appears when the data are
sorted after the fact using the later measurement. Nothing in the experiment
distinguishes a lazily evaluated universe from an ordinary quantum one.

**Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** It would, if it
said *when* rendering happens and at *what resolution*. The proposals surveyed
in chapter 4 (Campbell and colleagues) attempt to sharpen this into a test
[Campbell et al. 2017]. As it stands in the popular argument, it makes no
prediction that quantum mechanics does not already make.

**Grade.** B. The strongest of the physics hints in the sense that quantum
indeterminacy is genuinely strange and genuinely resembles lazy evaluation;
the weakest in the sense that the resemblance evaporates once "measurement" is
understood correctly.

## 2.1.4 Information as fundamental

**The clue.** The entropy of a black hole is proportional to the area of its
horizon, not its volume, which means the maximum information a region can hold
scales with its boundary (the Bekenstein bound and the holographic principle).
The universe, on this view, has a finite information content — Lloyd
estimated that the observable universe can have performed at most about
10¹²⁰ elementary operations since the Big Bang, on about 10⁹⁰ bits registered
in matter [Lloyd 2002], [Lloyd 2006].
Wheeler's slogan "it from bit" proposes that information is what the world is
made of [Wheeler 1990]. If the universe is finite information, it fits in a
finite memory.

**Is it true?** The black-hole entropy result is well established
theoretically, and the holographic principle is widely accepted as a
constraint that any theory of quantum gravity must satisfy, though it has not
been directly tested. Lloyd's counting is an upper bound, not a measurement.

**Does standard physics produce it?** Yes — this *is* standard physics, or at
least standard theoretical physics. Bekenstein's bound follows from
thermodynamics and general relativity. It says nothing about who, if anyone,
is holding the information.

**Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** It predicts that the
bound is *the size of the memory*, which would in turn predict that the bound
is saturated, or nearly, somewhere. The actual information content of ordinary
regions of space is enormously below the Bekenstein bound; only black holes
saturate it. A simulator provisioning memory would not allocate 10¹²⁰ bits to
store 10⁹⁰. The finite-information result is therefore consistent with
simulation but not suggestive of it.

**The infodynamics claim.** Melvin Vopson has proposed a "second law of
infodynamics" under which information entropy tends to decrease over time
[Vopson & Lepadatu 2022], and has argued that this is evidence of a
simulation optimising its data [Vopson 2023]. The proposal has appeared in a
peer-reviewed venue but has not been taken up by the physics community. No
independent group has reproduced it; the one peer-reviewed response argues
that the statistical-entropy basis "is ill defined, and it is not suitable to
define a physical law" [Crecraft 2025]; and the author has since published a
correction acknowledging a weakness in the cosmological derivation
[Vopson 2025]. It is recorded here as a claim to monitor, not as a result.
Grade E until independently reproduced.

**Grade.** B. Digital physics and the simulation hypothesis are routinely
conflated on exactly this point (chapter 1.2): "the universe is information"
is a claim about the universe, not about a computer somewhere else.

## 2.1.5 Fine-tuning

**The clue.** Several constants of nature — the cosmological constant most
famously, which is smaller than naive estimates by a factor of around
10¹²⁰ — have values that seem improbably well suited to producing a universe
with stars, chemistry and life. A designer would choose such values.

**Is it true?** The apparent fine-tuning is real in the sense that small
changes to some constants would produce a very different universe. Whether it
is *improbable* depends on a probability distribution over possible constants,
and we do not have one.

**Does standard physics produce it?** Not on its own. The main candidate
explanations are: a multiverse in which the constants vary and we necessarily
find ourselves in a region compatible with our existence (the anthropic
principle); a deeper theory in which the constants are not free; or design.

**Does the simulation reading predict anything further?** Here the simulation
reading has a specific weakness. If the simulators can choose any constants
they like, fine-tuning for *life* is predicted only if the simulators wanted
life — which is one purpose among many (chapter 1.2.3). And the simulators'
own universe must permit them to exist and build computers, so its constants
are fine-tuned too. The simulation reading pushes the fine-tuning question up
one level rather than answering it. The multiverse reading, whatever its other
problems, at least dissolves the question at the level where it is asked.

**Grade.** B, and arguably lower. Fine-tuning is evidence for *some*
explanation beyond brute fact; it does not favour simulation over the
alternatives and may disfavour it, since simulation multiplies the thing to be
explained.

## 2.1.6 Glitches and anomalies

**The clue.** Software has bugs. If the universe is software, some of the
tensions in physics might be bugs.

**Is it true that there are anomalies?** Yes. Two prominent ones at the time
of writing. The *Hubble tension*: the expansion rate inferred from the early
universe is 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc [Planck 2020], while the 2026 community
consensus from the local distance ladder is 73.50 ± 0.81 km/s/Mpc, a
disagreement of about seven standard deviations [H0DN 2026]. Observations
with the James Webb Space Telescope have not resolved it; they removed one
suspected systematic and left the two camps — one anchored on Cepheids, one on
red-giant and carbon-star methods giving an intermediate value near 70
[Freedman et al. 2025] — disagreeing about method rather than about
instruments. The *muon g-2 anomaly*: the measured magnetic moment of the muon
differed from the Standard Model prediction by about five standard deviations
as recently as 2023. The second is instructive. The 2025 final measurement
reached a precision of 127 parts per billion [Muon g-2 2025], and the 2025
theory consensus, which now computes the hadronic contribution with lattice
QCD rather than from older electron-positron data, agrees with it to within
0.6 standard deviations [Theory Initiative 2025]. The anomaly has not so much
been explained away as relocated: it is now a disagreement between two ways
of calculating the Standard Model value. That is what happens when an anomaly
is a limitation of our calculation rather than of nature.

**Does standard physics produce anomalies?** Constantly. The history of
physics is a history of anomalies that were resolved by better measurement,
better calculation, or new theory. Mercury's orbit was an anomaly until
general relativity. The solar neutrino deficit was an anomaly until neutrino
oscillation. An anomaly is the normal state of a frontier.

**What would a bug look like?** This is the question the glitch argument
never answers. A bug, to be recognisable as such, would have to be an event
that violates the laws of physics *as they actually are* — not as we currently
model them — in a way that is reproducible and localised. Nothing of the kind
has been observed. Every anomaly on record is either a disagreement between
two of our own measurements or between our measurements and our own
calculations. "Nature disagrees with our model" is not "nature disagrees with
itself".

**Personal glitches.** Set aside in chapter 2.3 and set aside again here:
unreproducible, unwitnessed, and fully accounted for by the known error rates
of human memory and perception.

**Grade.** B, at the bottom of the grade. The argument amounts to "physics
is unfinished", which is true and uninformative.

## 2.1.7 Complexity results

**The clue, inverted.** Zohar Ringel and Dmitry Kovrizhin showed that certain
quantum systems — those exhibiting a gravitational anomaly, as in some
quantum-Hall phases — cannot be simulated efficiently by any classical
algorithm of the quantum-Monte-Carlo type: the resources required grow
exponentially with the system size, and the obstruction (the "sign problem")
cannot be removed by a change of basis [Ringel & Kovrizhin 2017]. The result
was widely reported as a proof that the universe cannot be a simulation.

**Is it true?** The theorem is true. The headline is not what it says. The
result concerns *classical* simulation of specific quantum systems using a
specific class of methods. It says nothing about simulation on a quantum
computer, and nothing about simulation by a computer operating under physical
laws different from ours. The authors said as much.

**What it does establish.** Any simulation of our universe that is faithful at
the level of quantum many-body physics is *expensive*, and a simulator running
it on classical hardware would face exponential costs. This is evidence about
the *type* of simulation (chapter 1.2.2): it favours lazy, coarse, or
quantum-substrate simulations over full classical ones. It is not evidence
about whether simulation is happening.

**Grade.** Not an argument for the hypothesis; recorded here because it is
often cited in the debate, and because it feeds chapter 3.1 (the cost
objection).

## Predictions

- **If simulated on a finite cubic lattice:** cubic anisotropy in the
  highest-energy cosmic rays. *Proposed in 2012; no dedicated search
  published. Indirectly constrained by Lorentz-invariance bounds and by the
  observed dipole.*
- **If lazily rendered:** artefacts at the moment resolution is raised. *Not
  yet tested in a form both sides accept; chapter 4.*
- **If not simulated, or simulated with physics unlike ours:** no signature
  of any of the above. *Consistent with all observations to date.*

## Evidence for

- Quantum indeterminacy is genuinely unlike classical physics and genuinely
  resembles deferred computation. (Grade B.)
- The universe has a finite information content and finite computational
  capacity. (Grade B; also true of an unsimulated universe.)
- Fine-tuning needs *some* explanation. (Grade B; does not favour simulation
  specifically.)

## Evidence against

- The one worked-out physical prediction (lattice anisotropy) has not been
  directly tested, and is indirectly constrained by Lorentz-invariance bounds
  that already exclude a coarse grid.
- Lorentz invariance, tested to beyond the Planck energy, is the opposite of
  what a grid-based simulation would naturally produce.
- No anomaly on record has the form a bug would take.
- Simulation multiplies the fine-tuning problem rather than solving it.

## Provisional verdict

No feature of known physics looks more like a computational artefact than
like a brute fact or a consequence of deeper theory. Of the six clues, one
(discreteness) generated a real prediction that has never been directly
tested and is indirectly constrained; one (the speed limit) points the wrong
way; two (measurement, information) rest on conflating physics with a gloss
on physics; one (fine-tuning) is evidence for an explanation but not for this
one; and one (anomalies) is not evidence at all.

The physics case for the hypothesis is therefore **grade B at best, with
several items weakened to the point of contributing nothing**. This does not
count against the hypothesis — a high-fidelity simulation predicts exactly
this — but it means the case must be carried by chapter 2.2.

Confidence: high on the individual assessments. The status of the two
anomalies in 2.1.6 was verified against the 2025–2026 literature and will
need rechecking as that literature moves.
