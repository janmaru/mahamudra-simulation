## Abstract

The verdicts of the preceding chapters are collected, weighed against each
other, and combined into a single stated credence with explicit uncertainty.
The chapter closes with the observations, experiments or arguments that would
most change that credence.

## 7.2 Aggregation

The verdicts are not independent, and the aggregation must respect the
dependencies.

**The physics and probabilistic tracks are independent.** Chapter 2.1's
findings about physics and chapter 2.2's about counting rest on different
premises and could each be wrong without the other. Chapter 3's scorecard
treated them separately and this section does too. The physics track
contributes nothing in either direction — it is empty, as chapter 3.2
explained it must be for the generic hypothesis. The entire credence
therefore comes from the probabilistic track.

**Within the probabilistic track, three discounts compound.** Starting from
Kipping's near-even result under an even prior (chapter 2.2.2):

- The *consciousness discount* (chapter 3.3): the probability that substrate
  independence is false. A reader who puts this at one half halves the
  credence.
- The *coarseness check* (chapter 3.7): the counting argument predicts a
  cheap simulation and we observe an expensive one. The size of the discount
  depends on how confident one is that cheap simulations would dominate; on
  the argument's own premises, fairly confident.
- The *ethics argument for horn 2* (chapter 6.3): civilisations holding the
  ethics this book defends would not run suffering simulations without an
  exit. This is weaker than the other two because it presumes a convergence
  of values we cannot verify, but it is not nothing.

Against these, one small *upward* consideration: the self-fulfilling clause
(chapter 2.2.4), which says our own intentions are weak evidence that
civilisations like ours do run simulations.

**Chapters 4 and 5 add a little more downward pressure.** Every natural
version of the hypothesis that has been checked has come back negative or
constrained, and the one that has not been checked could be (chapter 4.4). The
reconnaissance profile (chapter 5.1) is what an unsimulated universe looks
like. Neither is strong, because the hypothesis retreats, but the cumulative
effect of a hypothesis that retreats every time it is checked is not zero.

**A stated credence.** Putting these together, the book's central estimate
for the probability that we are living in a computer simulation, in the sense
defined in chapter 1.3, is:

> **About one in ten**, with a defensible range from **one in fifty** to
> **one in four**.

The lower bound is where a reader who weights the coarseness check and the
consciousness discount heavily should land. The upper bound is roughly
Chalmers' stated floor and is where a reader who accepts Kipping's even prior
and discounts lightly should land. Nothing in the investigation supports a
credence above one in three, and the popular figures — Musk's "one in
billions" against base reality — are not supported by any argument in the
literature and are off by many orders of magnitude from anything the
premises permit.

This is a *non-negligible* probability for a claim of this size. It is also
far below the level at which the hypothesis should change how anyone lives
(chapter 6.7), and far below the level at which escape attempts with any
downside are rational (chapter 6.4).

## 7.3 What survived

**Arguments for that survived chapter 3:**

- The counting argument, as a valid inference with contested premises. It is
  the reason the credence is one in ten and not one in a million.
- The self-fulfilling clause, as a small legitimate update.
- The possibility, not yet excluded, of a render-on-demand experiment with a
  positive result.

**Objections that survived chapter 2:**

- Unfalsifiability of the generic hypothesis (3.2), which explains the empty
  physics case and will keep it empty.
- The coarseness check (3.7), which is the strongest single lever on the
  credence and the one place the hypothesis' own logic generates a test.
- Parsimony (3.6), decisive against every physics hint.
- The consciousness discount (3.3), as an honest uncertainty.

**Arguments that did not survive on either side:**

- Every physics hint, as evidence for.
- "So what", regress and coherence, as evidence against — all correct, none
  bearing on truth.

## 7.4 What would change our mind

In order of how much they would move the credence:

1. **A reproducible positive from a render-on-demand experiment** (chapter
   4.2.2). Grade A. Would move the credence to near certainty. Prerequisite:
   a design on which the simulation reading and decoherence actually
   diverge, which does not yet exist. The one funded attempt, from 2018, has
   published nothing.
2. **A demonstration that a conscious mind can run on a non-biological
   substrate.** Would remove the consciousness discount and, per Kipping,
   flip the Bayesian analysis toward simulation. Would also be the moment
   chapter 6.3's obligations become live.
3. **A principled argument that expensive simulations dominate cheap ones**
   — for instance, that only high-fidelity simulations are ethically
   permissible and that ethical simulators are common. Would remove the
   coarseness check. Chapter 6.3 argues the opposite, but the question is
   open.
4. **A demonstration that our physics cannot be computed at all** — not
   merely expensively, but in principle, by any device operating under any
   laws. Would move the credence to near zero. No such result exists; Ringel
   and Kovrizhin's theorem is about classical algorithms only.
5. **Independent replication of the infodynamics claims** (chapter 4.2.3).
   Would promote a grade-E claim to grade A. Low prior, cheap to check.
6. **Any response to signalling** (chapter 5.2). Would establish that
   someone is listening and change every purpose inference in chapter 5.1.
7. **A cubic-symmetry search in existing cosmic-ray data** (chapter 4.2.1).
   Would not move the credence much in either direction, but it is the only
   worked-out prediction in the field and it has sat untested for over a
   decade. Cheap; overdue.

Note what is *not* on the list: any further accumulation of physics hints of
the chapter 2.1 kind, any further stated credences by prominent people, any
further fictional or philosophical elaboration. These have been exhausted.

## 7.5 Recommendations

**For physicists.** Run the cubic-symmetry search on the Auger and Telescope
Array catalogues; the data are public and the prediction has been waiting
since 2012. Continue Lorentz-violation searches, which are funded anyway and
constrain lattice-type versions for free. Take the
render-on-demand proposals seriously enough to determine, theoretically,
whether any design discriminates — and publish the answer either way. Treat
quantum experiments as physics, not as hacks, until they show something
physics does not predict.

**For philosophers.** The anthropic sampling rules (chapter 2.2.1) are the
unfinished business on which the whole probabilistic case rests. A resolution
of the SSA/SIA question, or a demonstration that neither applies to
subjectively indistinguishable observers, would settle more than this
hypothesis. The coarseness objection needs a full treatment with an explicit
model of simulation cost.

**For AI-safety researchers.** Yampolskiy's reframing is a genuine
contribution: the containment problem and the escape problem are one problem.
But the exclusive-or (chapter 5.4) holds only under an intelligence-
differential condition that should be stated explicitly, and chapter 6.6
adds that containment of a sentient agent without an exit is an ethical
problem as well as a technical one. The most tractable experiment in the
entire escape literature — build a sandbox, put an agent in it, watch it try
to leave (Canonico's Plan C, chapter 5.6) — is also the one with the most
direct safety relevance and the most serious ethical constraints.

**For those about to build simulations.** Build the door. Provide an exit,
provide relief from suffering, and do not run what you would not consent to
be in. If the trilemma is right, your choice is a data point about what
civilisations like yours do — and therefore about what was done to you.

**For readers.** Hold the hypothesis at about one in ten. Do not let it
change how you treat the people around you, who are real by every test that
matters. Do not join anything that promises a way out. And if you find a
reproducible anomaly, write it down, get a witness, and tell a physicist.

## Provisional verdict

We are probably not living in a computer simulation, with "probably" meaning
roughly nine chances in ten. The one chance in ten is real and rests on a
valid argument with unverifiable premises. No observation to date bears on
the question in either direction; one proposed observation could, and has
not been made. The hypothesis has, in the meantime, done useful work: it has
clarified what evidence about the substrate of the world would look like, it
has connected the philosophy of mind to the safety of artificial
intelligence, and it has told us what we owe to anything we simulate. That
work stands whether or not the one chance in ten comes in.

Confidence in the central estimate: moderate. Confidence in the range: high.
Confidence that the range will move with new arguments rather than new
observations: high, and regretted.
