# 1.2 Types of Simulation

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

"Simulation" covers very different scenarios, and the evidence that would
support one may be irrelevant to another. This sub-chapter builds a taxonomy
along three axes — what is simulated, how faithfully, and why — and notes for
each type what observations it would make more or less likely.

## Question under investigation

Which kind of simulation are we asking about, and does the answer change what
counts as evidence?

## 1.2.1 Axis one: what is simulated

The first distinction is between simulations that compute the agents and
simulations that only compute the environment.

**Full simulation.** Environment and inhabitants are both computed. Our
bodies, our brains and therefore our minds are part of the program. This is
Bostrom's ancestor simulation, and it is what Yampolskiy calls a
*full-simulation* [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1]. Chalmers uses the term *pure
simulation* for the same case: all the beings inside are simulated beings
[Chalmers 2022].

**Partial simulation.** The environment is computed but the agents are not.
Minds that exist outside the simulation are connected to it and receive its
outputs as their sensory input — the *Matrix* scenario, or an extremely good
virtual-reality headset. Yampolskiy's *partial-simulation*; Chalmers'
*impure simulation* [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1], [Chalmers 2022]. Putnam's brain
in a vat is the limiting case in which there is one external mind.

The distinction matters for two reasons. First, the evidence differs. In a
partial simulation the agents are real physical objects somewhere, and the
simulation must interface with them; interfaces have bandwidth limits and
latencies that might, in principle, be detectable. In a full simulation there
is no interface, only computation, and the only traces are the ones the
computation itself leaves. Second, the exit differs. Yampolskiy observes that
in a partial simulation, ending the simulation — by shutdown, or by the death
of the avatar — returns the agent to base reality, because the agent was never
inside in the first place. In a full simulation, shutdown ends the agent
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1, n. 5]. This is not a small difference.

A third possibility is worth naming because it is implicit in Bostrom's
cost-saving remarks: a **selective simulation**, in which some inhabitants are
fully computed minds and others are "shadow people", rendered only as far as
needed to make the environment convincing to the real ones [Bostrom 2003].
This lowers the cost of the simulation and lowers the count of conscious
observers in it, which in turn affects the arithmetic of the previous
sub-chapter.

## 1.2.2 Axis two: fidelity and resolution

The second distinction concerns how much of the simulated world is actually
computed, and how accurately.

At one extreme, a simulation computes the full physics of every particle in
the observable universe at every instant. This is astronomically expensive and,
as chapter 3 will discuss, may be impossible in principle for a classical
computer if the physics is quantum. At the other extreme, a simulation
computes only what is observed, at the resolution at which it is observed, and
fills in the rest on demand. Bostrom explicitly describes this "lazy" strategy
as the one a sensible simulator would adopt [Bostrom 2003].

The choice on this axis determines what kind of glitch to look for. A
fully-resolved simulation on a discrete lattice leaves the lattice as its
signature — a preferred set of directions in space, detectable in the highest-
energy phenomena [Beane, Davoudi & Savage 2012]. A lazy simulation leaves
different marks: inconsistencies between what was rendered at low resolution
and what is later examined closely, or delays and artefacts at the moment
resolution is raised. Several of the experiments surveyed in chapter 4 are
attempts to catch a lazy renderer in the act.

Fidelity also bears on **nesting**. If a simulation is good enough to contain
civilisations that build computers, those civilisations may run simulations of
their own, and so on. Each level must be paid for by the level above, so the
total compute available to a nested simulation falls rapidly with depth, and
the expected number of levels below any given base reality is small
[Yampolskiy 2022, ref. 85]. Yampolskiy notes that a successful escape from a
nested simulation might land the escapee not in base reality but in the next
sandbox up, and that the two cases might be impossible to tell apart from
inside [Yampolskiy 2022, §2]. Chapter 2.2 uses the same cost argument to bound
the counting argument.

Chalmers adds two further fidelity-related distinctions [Chalmers 2022]. A
simulation may be **global** or **local**: the whole universe, or only a region
of it, with the rest faked at the boundary. And it may be **perfect** or
**imperfect**: indistinguishable from the world it models, or detectably
different. A perfect simulation is, by definition, untestable from inside, and
the question of whether ours is perfect is therefore the question of whether
this book can succeed.

## 1.2.3 Axis three: purpose

The third axis is why the simulation exists. This is the axis on which
Yampolskiy concentrates, because purpose predicts *security posture*: a
simulation built to contain something dangerous will be built differently
from one built for fun [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1].

The purposes proposed in the literature fall into a few groups.

**Research.** Bostrom's ancestor simulation is a historical or scientific
tool: the simulators want to know how their past went, or how it could have
gone. Related purposes include testing hypotheses in social science,
economics, or evolutionary biology, and exploring counterfactual histories.
Research simulations are likely to be numerous and to be run without much
attention to any individual inhabitant.

**Entertainment.** The simulation is a game, a story, or a spectacle.
Yampolskiy also lists the possibility of a *qualia mining* simulation, run to
generate a large variety of experiences from which the best are selected and
re-created for the simulators' own enjoyment [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1,
ref. 95]. Entertainment simulations, he argues, are unlikely to have serious
security and would be the easiest to escape.

**Containment, training or evaluation.** The simulation is a box. Its purpose
is to hold, test, train or handicap intelligent agents who might be dangerous
if let out — a prison, a rehabilitation facility, a sandbox for evaluating an
AI before deployment [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1, refs. 89–93]. This class is the
mirror image of the AI-containment problem in AI safety, and it is the one in
which the simulators have the strongest interest in making escape impossible.

**By-product.** The simulation was not built for its inhabitants at all. If
the simulators are themselves artificial intelligences, our universe might be
an intermediate step in some larger computation — an attempt to model human
preferences, say — that happens to contain conscious beings as a side effect
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1, refs. 96–98]. In this case nobody is watching, the
purpose predicts nothing about security, and the simulation may be terminated
the moment the larger computation no longer needs it.

**Escape room.** Yampolskiy floats the possibility that the simulation is
*designed* to be escaped: a puzzle whose solution is the exit, with the side
benefit of identifying agents capable of solving it [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1,
n. 4]. This is speculation, and the text marks it as such; it is recorded
because it is the one purpose under which looking for the exit is exactly
what the designers intend.

## 1.2.4 Cross-tabulation

The three axes are independent, and the space they define is large. The table
below records, for the combinations that recur in the literature, what
signature each would leave and how hard each would be to leave.

| Type | Agents | Fidelity | Purpose | Expected signature | Expected security |
|------|--------|----------|---------|--------------------|-------------------|
| Ancestor simulation | Full | Lazy, selective | Research | Rendering artefacts; possibly lattice | Low to moderate; the inhabitants are not the point |
| Game / spectacle | Full or partial | Lazy | Entertainment | Narrative coherence; "interesting" events over-represented | Low |
| Containment box | Full | High where it matters | Security | Few glitches; hard limits on capability | High |
| Training sandbox | Full | Variable | Evaluation | Scenario structure; repeated trials | High during trial |
| By-product | Full | Whatever the outer task needs | None | Nothing aimed at us | Undefined; termination risk |
| Matrix / VR | Partial | High at the interface | Various | Interface latency and bandwidth | Depends on purpose |
| Escape room | Full | High | Selection | Deliberate clues | Designed to be defeated |

Two things stand out. First, the *signature* column is mostly about fidelity,
not purpose: what we can hope to observe depends on how the simulation is
computed, not why. Second, the *security* column is mostly about purpose:
whether we could ever do anything about it depends on why, not how. This
division of labour is why the book separates the question of *whether*
(chapters 2 to 4, driven by axis two) from the question of *what then*
(chapters 5 and 6, driven by axis three).

## 1.2.5 The type this book focuses on

The rest of the book concentrates on **full, high-fidelity simulations of
unknown purpose**, for three reasons.

It is the case Bostrom's argument is about, so it is the case for which a
probabilistic argument exists.

It is the hardest case for the investigator. A partial simulation has an
interface; a low-fidelity simulation has artefacts. A full, high-fidelity
simulation offers the fewest handholds, so any evidence that survives in that
case survives in the others.

And it is the case in which the question of purpose is genuinely open, which
keeps chapters 5 and 6 honest: we do not get to assume the simulators are
watching, or that they are friendly, or that they are absent.

Where a result transfers to other types, the text says so. Where it depends
on the type, the text says that too.

## Predictions

- **If the simulation is purpose-built for containment or training**, we
  should expect few exploitable glitches and firm limits on what can be
  built or computed inside it. The existence of hard physical limits (the
  speed of light, the Bekenstein bound) is consistent with this but is also
  consistent with ordinary physics.
- **If the simulation is for entertainment or research**, we should expect
  cost-saving shortcuts, and therefore the rendering artefacts that chapters
  2.1 and 4 look for.
- **If the simulation is a by-product**, we should expect nothing aimed at us
  at all, and the only relevant prediction is about termination risk
  (chapter 4.5).

## Provisional verdict

The taxonomy shows that "are we in a simulation?" is at least a dozen
questions. The evidence available from inside bears on fidelity; the question
of what to do bears on purpose; and the two axes are nearly independent. A
verdict on the hypothesis as a whole will therefore have to be a verdict on
a *distribution* over types, not a single yes or no.

Confidence: high that the axes are the right ones; moderate that the table's
entries are correct, since several rest on a single source.

## Open threads

- Can a partial simulation be distinguished from a full one from inside? If
  the interface is good enough, perhaps not; but the interface must exist,
  and existing things have properties.
