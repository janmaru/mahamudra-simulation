# 1. The Simulation Hypothesis

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

This chapter traces the idea from its philosophical ancestors, Plato's cave,
Zhuangzi's butterfly, Descartes' deceiver, Putnam's brain in a vat, to its
modern computational form.

## Question under investigation

What exactly does "we live in a simulation" assert?

## 1.1 Ancestors of the idea

The idea that the world we perceive is not the real world is a quiet very
old idea. 


**Plato's cave.** In Book VII of the *Republic*, prisoners chained in a cave
see only the shadows cast on a wall by objects passing a fire.
The shadows become their reality. 
Once a prisoner is freed, she sees the fire and then the sun, and returns to tell the others, who do not believe him [Plato]. 
Nobody built the cave in order to fool its occupants. 
But if go back to the old question about if math has been discovered or invented, we noticed that some archetypes are there in order to be grasped.
Who created this generic concept that in some way we're bound to uknowledge?
If the fabric of the universe is discrete then it points out to a preconstructed, predesigned universe.

**Zhuangzi's butterfly.** In the second chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, the author
dreams he is a butterfly, wakes, and cannot tell whether he is Zhuang Zhou who
dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhuang Zhou
[Zhuangzi]. If the very frabic of reality could be questioned but a simple paradox, what to say about reality?

**Descartes' deceiver.** In his meditation, Descartes imagines an agent with an intention
creating and arranging everything he perceives, the sky,
earth, colours, shapes, his own body forcing to realize that is illusion [Descartes 1641]. 
Descartes introduced the idea that a deceiver could fake everything but his own thinking. 
The sentiment of it, is that looking for a fixed point that
survives any inquiry could be found.

**Putnam's brain in a vat.** Hilary Putnam in the twentieth-
century images a brain kept alive in a tank, wired to a computer that feeds it
exactly the sensory inputs a body in would find like in our world. [Putnam 1981].
It is the first version that implies that a *computer* could be seriously the source of our illusion.


**Ramana Maharshi** In the non-dual Indian philosophy of Advaita Vedanta, the physical world we perceive is illusion (Maya—an) that obscures the ultimate reality (Brahman). In the 20th century, Ramana Maharshi crystallized this into a direct method called Self-enquiry. He proposed that the entire illusion of the external world is anchored by a single point: the ego, or the fundamental "I-thought." By constantly turning the mind inward and relentlessly asking "Who am I?", the seeker traces the mind back to its source. In doing so, the separate self dissolves, revealing that the observer and the observed are one. 

These stories share a common insight: a perceived world, that is imperfect through our senses, could be known from the inside.
What is left, is any account of *why* the mechanism
would exist, *what it would cost*, or *how likely* it is. 

## 1.2 The computational turn

The first serious proposal that physics comes out of a computational device came from Konrad Zuse,
the German engineer who built one of the first programmable computers. 
In *Rechnender Raum* — "Calculating Space" — he suggested that the universe might
be a cellular automaton, a grid of cells each updated by a simple rule [Zuse 1969].

Edward Fredkin developed the same idea under the name *digital
physics* or *digital mechanics*, arguing that a finite, discrete, deterministic
computational process could underlie all of physics [Fredkin 1990]. 

John Wheeler summarised a related but distinct view in the slogan "it from bit":
every physical quantity derives its meaning from yes-or-no answers to
questions, so information, not matter, is fundamental [Wheeler 1990].

The claim the universe *being* computation, or information does not simplfy in *the universe as a program someone is running* or the simulation hypothesis.

Hans Moravec, a roboticist, argued in *Mind Children* that a mind is a pattern that could be
copied off its biological substrate and run elsewhere, and that a civilisation
with enough computing power could run whole simulated worlds populated by such
minds [Moravec 1988]. This supplied the missing motive: a simulation is
something a technological civilisation might *want* to run, and might be
*able* to run.

The third ingredient was Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper, which combined the two and
added arithmetic. If minds can run on computers, and if advanced civilisations
would have both the capacity and the inclination to run many simulations of
their ancestors, then simulated minds would outnumber original minds by a
large factor, and a mind that did not know which kind it was should bet on
being simulated [Bostrom 2003]. The argument is examined in detail in the next
sub-chapter. Its importance here is that it converted a sceptical scenario into
a claim with premises that can be true or false, and therefore into a
hypothesis that can be investigated.

## 1.3 A working definition

For the rest of this book:

> **The simulation hypothesis** is the claim that the physical universe we
> observe, including its laws and its history, is being computed by a process
> that runs on a substrate outside that universe, and that the process was
> initiated by one or more agents.

Three clauses do the work.

*Computed.* The universe is the output of a process that could in principle be
described as an algorithm operating on a state. This is stronger than "the
universe is describable by mathematics" — every physicist believes that — and
stronger than digital physics, because it requires the computation to be
*happening somewhere*.

*Outside.* The substrate is not part of the observable universe. This rules
out the trivial sense in which a weather model "simulates" the atmosphere from
inside the atmosphere, and it is what gives the hypothesis its bite: there is
a level of reality we do not have direct access to.

*Agents.* Someone started it. This distinguishes the hypothesis from the view
that the universe is a computation in the way a river is a computation — a
natural process that happens to be describable that way — and it is the clause
that makes chapters 5 and 6 possible. Without agents there is nobody to
communicate with, nobody to blame, and nobody to escape to.

Two further distinctions must be kept clear, because the literature does not
always keep them.

**The hypothesis versus the argument.** The *simulation hypothesis* is the
claim above. The *simulation argument* is Bostrom's specific inference that at
least one of three propositions is true, one of which is the hypothesis
[Bostrom 2003]. The argument can be valid while the hypothesis is false; the
hypothesis can be true while the argument is unsound. Bostrom himself has
repeatedly pointed out that he does not claim we are simulated, only that the
trilemma holds.

**The hypothesis versus its neighbours.** Idealism holds that reality is
mental; the simulation hypothesis is compatible with a fully physical base
reality. The multiverse holds that many universes exist; the simulation
hypothesis holds that at least one universe *contains* another, which is a
different relation. Max Tegmark's Mathematical Universe Hypothesis holds that
physical existence and mathematical existence are the same thing
[Tegmark 2014]; on that view a simulated universe and an unsimulated one with
the same mathematical structure would be *identical*, which dissolves rather
than answers our question. These neighbours will be visited again when the
evidence is weighed, because arguments for them are frequently borrowed as
arguments for simulation.

## 1.4 What the hypothesis does and does not imply

The definition is deliberately minimal, and it is worth listing what it leaves
open, because popular discussion tends to fill the gaps with the furniture of
a particular film.

It **does not** imply that the simulators are gods in any traditional sense.
They may be very powerful relative to us, but nothing in the hypothesis makes
them omniscient, omnipotent, benevolent, or even still present. A simulation
can be left running by someone who has forgotten it.

It **does not** imply that our bodies are real and only our sensory input is
faked, in the manner of *The Matrix*. That is one variant — chapter 1.2 will
call it a *partial* simulation — but the more commonly discussed variant has
our bodies, brains and minds computed along with everything else.

It **does not** imply that the simulation is *of* anything. Bostrom's version
is an "ancestor simulation", a model of the simulators' own past. But a
simulation can be a fiction, an experiment, a game, or a by-product.

It **does** imply finite resources. A computation has a cost, and whoever is
paying it has a budget. This is the single most productive consequence of the
hypothesis, because budgets leave traces: approximations, shortcuts, things
not rendered until needed. Chapters 2.1 and 4 are largely about looking for
those traces.

It **does** imply a purpose or an accident. Agents start processes for
reasons, or by mistake. Either way, the fact that the simulation exists is
itself a datum about the simulators, and chapter 5.1 will try to read it.

And it **does** imply the possibility of bugs. Every large program ever
written has contained errors. If the universe is a program, the prior that it
is bug-free is low, and the search for bugs is a legitimate research
programme — provided we can say in advance what a bug would look like and how
it would differ from ordinary physics we have not yet understood.

## 1.5 The popular version and its distortions

In June 2016 Elon Musk told an audience that the odds we are living in "base
reality" are "one in billions" [Musk 2016]. The remark was widely reported and
did more than any academic paper to put the idea into general circulation. It
is worth seeing what it compresses.

Musk's reasoning, as reported, ran: games forty years ago were two rectangles
and a dot; today they are photorealistic and played by millions; extrapolate
and games will be indistinguishable from reality; therefore there will be
billions of such realities and only one base reality; therefore the odds. This
is Bostrom's counting argument with the premises removed. It assumes the
extrapolation continues indefinitely, assumes the simulated players are
conscious, assumes the count of simulated worlds dominates without saying how
many base worlds there might be, and skips entirely the question of whether
the simulations would be *of us*. Each of those assumptions is a horn of the
trilemma or a premise of the argument, and each is contested in the literature.

The popular version therefore conflates at least three distinct claims:

1. Simulations indistinguishable from reality will eventually be possible.
2. They will be run in vast numbers.
3. Therefore we are probably in one.

Claim 1 is a prediction about technology. Claim 2 is a prediction about the
preferences of beings we know nothing about. Claim 3 does not follow from 1
and 2 without a principle about how to distribute credence across observers,
and that principle is where most of the philosophical objection concentrates.
The next sub-chapter takes these apart properly.

## Predictions

- **If the hypothesis is true:** the universe was built under constraints and
  should carry the marks of them — finite precision, resource optimisation,
  possibly errors. Chapter 2.1 investigates whether it does.
- **If the hypothesis is false:** the universe should show no marks beyond
  those that its own physics explains. Note that the two predictions overlap
  heavily, because a sufficiently good simulation shows no marks either. This
  overlap is the central methodological problem of the book and is confronted
  in chapters 3 and 4.

## Provisional verdict

The modern simulation hypothesis is genuinely different from its sceptical
ancestors. Plato, Zhuangzi, Descartes and Putnam ask whether we *could* be
deceived; Bostrom and his successors ask how *likely* it is, and supply
premises that can be examined. That shift makes the question empirical in
principle, which is what licenses the rest of this investigation.

Confidence in this verdict: high. It is a verdict about the *form* of the
hypothesis, not about its truth.
