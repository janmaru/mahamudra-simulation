# 5.1 Reconnaissance

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

Any attack begins by mapping the target. For a simulation the map has three
parts: its purpose, its type, and its resource envelope. This sub-chapter
follows Yampolskiy §3.1 and asks what can be inferred about each from the inside.

## Question under investigation

What can be learned about the simulation's purpose, type and budget without
leaving it?

## 5.1.1 Inferring purpose

Yampolskiy's first move is to note that purpose predicts *security posture*
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1]. A simulation that exists to contain something —
a prison, a rehabilitation programme, an evaluation sandbox for potentially
dangerous agents, a training environment, or a deliberate handicapping of
capable minds — would be built with security as a design goal, and escape
would be correspondingly hard. A simulation that exists for entertainment,
including the "qualia mining" variant in which experiences are generated in
bulk so the best can be selected and replayed, would have little reason to
invest in security and would be the easiest to leave. Scientific,
commercial, historical and training simulations fall in between. And if the
simulators are themselves AIs, the simulation may be a by-product of their
reasoning — an attempt to model human preferences, for instance — with no
security posture at all because nobody is treating it as a container.

He adds one more: the simulation may be an *escape room*, designed to be
solved, with the exit as the prize and the identification of agents capable
of finding it as the side benefit [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1, n. 4].

**Can purpose be read from inside?** Chapter 1.2.4 noted that the
observable signature of a simulation depends mostly on its *fidelity*, not
its purpose. The proxies for purpose that the paper and this book can
identify are indirect:

- *Suffering.* A simulation that contains large amounts of uncompensated
  suffering was built by simulators willing to include it. Yampolskiy draws
  the inference: whatever else our simulators are, they are "the type of
  simulators who are willing to include suffering of sentient-beings into
  their software" [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8]. This rules out purposes that
  require the inhabitants' welfare (a benevolent personal universe) and is
  consistent with research, entertainment, containment, and by-product.
- *Non-intervention.* Nothing anyone has said or done has produced a
  detectable response (chapter 4.3, 5.8). This is weak evidence against
  purposes that involve monitoring individual inhabitants — training,
  evaluation, escape room — and weak evidence for purposes that do not:
  research at the aggregate level, by-product, or an entertainment
  simulation whose audience is not interactive.
- *Interest.* Hanson's advice to "participate in pivotal events, be
  entertaining and praiseworthy" [Hanson 2001] presupposes an entertainment
  purpose. Yampolskiy's tongue-in-cheek "Elon's razor" — the most
  entertaining outcome is the most likely — is the same presupposition
  [Yampolskiy 2022, §6]. Whether history looks *engineered* for interest is
  a question with no agreed method of answering.
- *Fine-tuning for observers.* A universe that permits complex observers is
  consistent with any purpose that needs observers and inconsistent only
  with purposes that do not — which is nearly none.

The honest summary is that purpose is *underdetermined* by anything we can
observe. The two proxies with any force — suffering and non-intervention —
point toward a simulation that was not built for us and is not watching us
closely: research or by-product. That is also the kind with the least
deliberate security and the highest termination risk (chapter 4.5).

## 5.1.2 Inferring type

The full/partial distinction of chapter 1.2.1 matters for the exit:
in a partial simulation, shutdown or death returns the external agent to
base reality; in a full simulation, it ends the agent [Yampolskiy 2022,
§3.1, n. 5]. Can we tell which we are in?

Two considerations, neither decisive. First, a partial simulation must
interface with external minds, and the interface must carry every sensory
channel at full bandwidth with no detectable latency; everything we know
about the neuroscience of perception is consistent with our minds being
*inside* the physics, generated by brains that obey it. Second, the
population: a partial simulation with eight billion external participants
requires eight billion minds outside, which is a large commitment for the
simulators; a full simulation generates them for free. Neither consideration
excludes the partial case, but both make it the less parsimonious reading
*given* that we are simulated at all. This book's working assumption
(chapter 1.2.5) is a full simulation, and nothing in the reconnaissance
changes it.

## 5.1.3 Inferring the physics of base reality

Wei Dai proposes a prior over the physics of base reality: the probability
that a possible universe is base reality is inversely related to the
complexity of its laws, formalised as *P(X) = n^−K(X)*, where *K(X)* is the
length of the shortest definition of a set isomorphic to *X* in the language
of a formal set theory [Dai 2004, quoted in Yampolskiy 2022, §3.1]. The
generalisation to sets rather than programs is deliberate: it avoids
assuming that base reality is discrete or computable.

The prior has two uses for reconnaissance. It suggests that base reality is
*simple* — simpler than our universe, if ours is a simulation, since the
simulated is at least as complex as its description within the simulator's
physics. And it implies that whatever is outside is *describable*, which is
a precondition for the coherence objection of chapter 3.8 not being fatal.
What it does not give is any content: a simple base reality could have laws
utterly unlike ours, with more dimensions or fewer, different causality, or
no time in our sense. Dai's own remark that simulators may help us escape
"such as wanting someone to talk to or play with" [Dai 2018] presupposes a
psychology, which the prior does not license.

## 5.1.4 Inferring the budget

Gwern Branwen attempts to bound the computational resources of the simulating
universe from what our universe requires [Branwen 2012]. He obtains only a
very weak lower bound — the simulator must be at least large enough to hold
the state of our observable universe as rendered — and from it a very rough
estimate of the simulating universe's size. The interesting part of the
analysis is what follows: proposals to *raise* the bound by forcing the
simulators to spend more.

- **Von Neumann probes** sent to the far corners of the universe, forcing
  the rendering of regions that would otherwise be computed coarsely.
- **Simulations of our own**, adding layers of abstraction that the
  simulators cannot see through. Branwen invokes Rice's theorem: no general
  procedure can determine what an arbitrary program does, so the simulators
  cannot in general optimise away our nested computations by recognising
  their purpose. Each layer of interpretation "becomes ever more opaque,
  indirect, and bulky", and there could be dozens.
- **Brute force.** Dovetail through every possible program, or attack open
  problems like the Goldbach conjecture by exhaustive search. It is easy to
  waste indefinite computation, and every unit must be supplied by someone.

The paper adds the practical observation that none of this has been tried at
scale on purpose, and that the unintentional versions — bitcoin mining, the
LHC — have produced no visible strain [Yampolskiy 2022, §5]. Two readings are
available. The budget is enormous relative to anything we have done, in which
case resource attacks are futile. Or the simulation is lazy and *does not
compute what we do not observe*, in which case our computations are only as
expensive as their observable outputs, and mining bitcoin costs the
simulators no more than the rendering of some warm data centres. The second
reading is the cheaper design and therefore, on the coarseness logic of
chapter 3.7, the more likely.

## 5.1.5 Reconnaissance as an experiment programme

Most of the above is *inference* from what is already known. Yampolskiy's
"simulation forensics" — searching the universe for evidence of security
mechanisms, on the model of AI forensics and "designometry" — would be
*observation* [Yampolskiy 2022, §6]. What would a security mechanism look
like from inside?

- **Capability ceilings.** Hard limits on what can be built or computed:
  the speed of light, the Bekenstein bound, the no-cloning theorem, the
  uncomputability of certain functions. All of these exist. All of them are
  also consequences of ordinary physics and mathematics (chapter 2.1), and
  parsimony (chapter 3.6) prefers the ordinary reading. A limit is evidence
  of a *designed* limit only if it is arbitrary — if it sits where no
  physical principle requires it — and none of the known limits is.
- **Monitoring.** Any evidence that specific events or agents attract
  intervention. None has been found (5.1.1).
- **Patching.** Any evidence that the rules have changed. Physics appears to
  have been constant over 13.8 billion years to the precision of every test
  yet made, including tests of the fine-structure constant at high redshift.
  A patch that left no trace would be invisible; a patch that left a trace
  has not been seen.
- **Compartmentalisation.** In a lazily rendered simulation, the boundary
  between rendered and unrendered regions is where seams would be. This is
  the render-on-demand test of chapter 4.2.2, and it is the one item in the
  forensic programme with a plausible experiment attached.

The overlap with chapter 4 is nearly total: reconnaissance, done honestly,
*is* the search for evidence, and it reaches the same conclusion. The
probes that double as tests of the hypothesis are the observational ones;
the probes that are distinctively *attacks* — resource exhaustion — are
exactly the ones Greene's condition (chapter 4.5) counsels against.

## Predictions

- **If the simulation is purpose-built for containment**, reconnaissance
  should reveal arbitrary limits — ceilings that no physical principle
  requires. *None found.*
- **If the simulation is lazy and unmonitored**, reconnaissance should
  reveal nothing aimed at us, and resource attacks should produce no strain
  at any scale we can reach. *Consistent with observation.*
- **If the simulation is an escape room**, there should be deliberate clues.
  *None identified, though the class of "clue" is not well defined.*

## Provisional verdict

Reconnaissance from inside yields a *negative profile*: no evidence of
monitoring, no evidence of patching, no arbitrary limits, no response to
provocation, no strain under load. If we are simulated, the profile fits a
simulation that was not built for its inhabitants and is not watching them —
a research run or a by-product, lazily rendered, with a large budget relative
to our activity or none of our activity costing anything. That is the type
with the least deliberate security and the least reason for anyone outside to
help, which makes the assisted escape of 5.2 harder and the termination risk
of chapter 4.5 more salient.

None of this is evidence *for* the hypothesis. A negative profile is also
exactly what an unsimulated universe looks like.

Confidence: moderate. The inferences are sound but rest on a handful of weak
proxies, and "underdetermined" is the correct description of purpose.

## Open threads

- Is any proxy for purpose actually observable? The suffering proxy is the
  strongest and it excludes only benevolent purposes.
- Branwen's Rice's-theorem argument assumes the simulators cannot recognise
  our computations' purpose. A simulator who can read our *intentions*
  directly — they have our full state — does not need to analyse our
  programs. Does the argument survive that?
- If the negative profile fits "unmonitored research run", the ethical
  analysis of chapter 6 should be conducted on that assumption.
