# 6.1 Minimal Conditions of Experience

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

This sub-chapter examines a simple proposal: that experience requires three
conditions to remain non-zero — coherence, interaction and complexity. It asks
whether identity through change, and time as change measured against retained
reference, set minimal constraints on any world that contains observers. The
result is not a proof of consciousness, but a claim about the kind of
relational order a simulation must preserve if the beings inside it are to
count as continuing subjects rather than as disconnected snapshots.

## Question under investigation

Must a world that contains observers preserve coherence, interaction and
complexity strongly enough for identity and time to remain trackable from
within?

## 6.1.1 The proposed schema

Kodra's proposal can be written schematically as:

> **E = C × I × K ≠ 0**

where **E** is existence understood as experience, **C** is coherence,
**I** is interaction, and **K** is complexity [Kodra 2026]. The form should
not be read as a measured physical law. It is a compact way of stating a
minimal claim: if any one of the three terms vanishes, the conditions for a
point of view vanish with it.

The proposal is attractive because each term blocks a different failure mode.
Without coherence, there is no persisting pattern to which experience could be
ascribed. Without interaction, there is no contact with anything from which a
perspective could be formed. Without complexity, there may be change, but not
the articulated structure needed for memory, comparison or discrimination.

Read this way, the equation does not say that any sufficiently coherent,
interactive and complex system is conscious. It says only that a candidate
world lacking one of those features cannot obviously host experience at all.

## 6.1.2 Identity through change

The naive picture of identity says that an entity remains the same because
something beneath the changes does not change. Kodra's picture is the opposite:
an entity remains identifiable because its changes stay connected in a way that
makes re-identification possible [Kodra 2026].

Put in the book's terms, the subject passes through a sequence of states:

> **S1 → S2 → ... → Sn**

No two states need be identical. What matters is whether the transitions form a
sufficiently coherent chain. Identity is therefore not bare stasis but
structured continuity. The relevant question is not "what never changed?" but
"what preserved enough order across the changes for us to treat the later state
as the successor of the earlier one?"

This matters to the simulation hypothesis because many versions of it rely on
cost-saving shortcuts: selective rendering, lazy evaluation, state compression,
and abrupt updates when attention shifts [Bostrom 2003]. Those shortcuts are
compatible with continued experience only if the shortcuts preserve continuity
well enough that the subject's own history remains connected. A world that
replaces one observer-state with another without a coherent bridge may preserve
behaviour while failing to preserve the subject.

## 6.1.3 Time as change plus reference

Physical clocks do not explain time; they provide reproducible relations among
changing events. This raises two different questions. One is how time becomes
*measurable* through periodicity, invariance and repeatable comparison. The
other is how time becomes *experienceable* for a subject at all. Those are not
the same question, and the second is the more fundamental one here.

Change by itself does not yield duration unless some state is retained against
which later states can be compared, and comparison requires not only retention
but the recognition of a relation across states. In that sense, symmetry is not
just a feature exploited by clocks. The more important possibility is that the
capacity to recognize symmetry, recurrence or invariance is part of what makes
a sequence of changes intelligible as a temporal sequence from within.

The relevant internal capacity need not be a literal clock. It can be memory,
pattern recognition, anticipation of recurrence, or any mechanism by which the
system keeps enough of one state available to relate it to another
[Kodra 2026]. Time, from the inside, is therefore not merely change but change
organized into relations that remain available across successive states.

On this reading, symmetry is neither an intrinsic property of a single state
nor merely a projection of the mind onto otherwise unrelated events. It is a
relation between states, recognized by a system capable of preserving
information about them. Interaction creates those relations; coherence
preserves them; complexity allows increasingly sophisticated forms of
comparison. The equation **E = C × I × K ≠ 0** can then be reread as a claim
about the minimum architecture required for temporal experience, not merely for
bare existence.

This helps with an otherwise familiar puzzle. Intervals often feel long while
they are being lived through and short when later remembered, or the reverse.
The contradiction softens once one distinguishes lived duration from
reconstructed duration. The first depends on the organisation of ongoing
change; the second depends on what structure memory preserved of it. In both
cases, what matters is not change alone but the system's ability to preserve
and compare patterns within the change.

For the simulation hypothesis, the implication is direct. A simulation can be
cheap in many ways, but if it rewrites histories, drops intermediate states, or
fails to preserve enough trace for comparison, it threatens not only memory but
the subject's temporal standpoint. A simulated observer need not know absolute
time, but it must retain enough ordered change for experienced time to occur at
all.

## 6.1.4 What the schema does and does not show

The strongest caution in Kodra's proposal is that coherence alone does not
guarantee subjectivity [Kodra 2026]. A stable dynamical system can preserve its
state-transition structure without there being anything it is like to be that
system. The complexity term helps by ruling out trivial cases, but the proposal
does not specify the threshold at which complexity becomes enough for
experience.

This puts the schema in the same class as several arguments discussed earlier
in the book. It is a claim about necessary conditions, not sufficient ones.
Like Chalmers' realism about virtual worlds, it tells us what would have to be
true if simulated minds are real; unlike a full theory of consciousness, it
does not tell us when those conditions become enough [Chalmers 2022].

That limitation is a virtue in this context. The book does not need a complete
theory of consciousness. It needs a disciplined way to ask whether a putative
simulation architecture preserves enough relational continuity for observerhood
and temporal experience to survive.

## 6.1.5 Why this belongs in the book

This sub-chapter belongs here because it sharpens three questions the rest of
the book already depends on.

First, it gives chapter 3's consciousness discount a more precise shape. The
problem is not only whether consciousness is substrate-independent, but whether
a candidate substrate preserves the continuity conditions under which an
observer can remain a subject across time.

Second, it constrains chapter 4's and chapter 5's more economical simulation
models. Render-on-demand, selective simulation, memory compression and upload
scenarios are not neutral engineering choices. Each must answer whether the
coherent chain linking observer-states is preserved or merely imitated.

Third, it matters to chapter 6's ethics. If identity is continuity through
change rather than mere behavioural similarity, then a simulator who interrupts
or replaces a subject may be doing more than editing content; they may be
ending one subject and creating another. The ethics of escape, containment and
simulated suffering depends on that distinction.

It also offers a modest positive support for the simulation hypothesis. If
experience requires not absolute time but the preservation of structured
relations across states, then a simulation need not reproduce every detail of a
world continuously and globally. It must preserve the relational order that
makes identity and temporal continuity available to observers. That fits the
kind of economy Bostrom's hypothesis already expects: a simulation can be
selective, compressive or render-on-demand while still supporting genuine
experience, provided it does not break the coherent web of relations on which
experience depends [Bostrom 2003].

## Predictions

- **If observerhood requires continuity through change**, proposals for upload,
  restoration or escape must preserve a coherent chain of intermediate states;
  mere functional similarity at the endpoints is not obviously enough.
- **If time becomes experienceable through recognized relations across states**,
  a simulation may economise on low-level detail while preserving subjective
  continuity, so long as the relational structure available to the observer is
  maintained.
- **If experienced time requires change plus retained reference**, systems that
  cannot preserve memory, pattern or other internal comparison cannot possess
  duration from the inside, however many external changes occur around them.
- **If coherence, interaction and complexity are necessary but not sufficient**,
  discovering those three features in a system is not, by itself, evidence that
  the system is conscious.

## Provisional verdict

As a conceptual constraint, the schema is useful. Any version of the simulation
hypothesis that aims to contain genuine observers must preserve enough
coherence, interaction and complexity for identity and time to remain
trackable from within. The sharper claim inside that broader frame is that the
recognition of relations across states — one form of symmetry-recognition — may
be part of what makes time show up as time for a subject at all.

That is enough for the book's purposes. The proposal does not prove the
hypothesis, but it does offer a limited support to one family of simulated-world
models: those that preserve relational continuity rather than brute
state-by-state fullness. It therefore clarifies what a successful simulation of
conscious life would have to preserve, and what would be at stake in escape,
uploading, restoration and interruption.

Confidence: moderate. The argument is conceptually strong, but the threshold
for "enough" coherence or complexity is left open.

## Open threads

- How much discontinuity can a subject tolerate before identity fails: none,
  some, or only discontinuity without recoverable trace?
- Is symmetry itself the right concept here, or would recurrence, invariance or
  relational comparability name the condition more precisely?
- Can the continuity condition be stated in functional terms alone, or does it
  depend on the physical details of the substrate?
- If time for an observer requires retained reference, how should chapter 4's
  render-on-demand proposals distinguish harmless compression from destructive
  rewriting?
