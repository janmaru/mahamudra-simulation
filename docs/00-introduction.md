# 0. Introduction: Why Investigate, and How

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

The simulation hypothesis is usually treated as either a party trick or a matter of faith. This book treats it as a case to be investigated. This introduction sets the question, explains why it is worth the effort, and describes the method every chapter follows.

## 0.1 The question

The question under investigation in this book is:

> Is the reality we live in a computer simulation?

The hypothesis says that the physical universe we observe, its particles, fields,
spacetime and the laws that govern them, is not primary. It is a process
being computed on some stack that lies outside the universe we can see, by
someone or something that built the computation and, possibly, still runs it.
The ideas in psychology we have been acquainted with are among the forces that drive humans in the simulation.

Some things are *set aside*.

We do not care whether the idea might be disturbing to some people. People's reactions could be counted as evidence only when
put in sociological or psychological terms. In that case people's feelings would be measured like any other parameter.

We are not bothered by the fact that the reason for the inquiry might be read as insincere since it is fashionable nowadays. Of course fashion is not
evidence in itself.

The religious hypothesis can be viewed as a possible fact that would make the simulation hypothesis true, and we will discuss it later (the possibility of transcendence; and chapter 6 will look at those resonances), but we are not going to cherry-pick one or the other, it is obviously irrelevant to the quest we are in.
And we are not asking whether the hypothesis is compatible with any particular religion unless it has a resonance also in philosophy.

## 0.2 Why it matters

**If the hypothesis is true, "god" exists.**
A simulated universe runs on something. This something, which from now on will be called "outre-mer", contains the processes that
built our lives, the meaning and computational resources vastly larger
than ours. 
Also the origin of the universe, the nature of consciousness, whether
there is a purpose to any of it.
We could argue that the very idea of a simulated world is compelling evidence to justify the philosophical effort humankind has put into answering the fundamental questions, since it is some kind of normal outcome of any intelligent agent [Yampolskiy 2022].

**If the hypothesis is false, life is not worth living.**
A lot of studies on the future of computing and the role of AI in our society rest on the
premise that simulations of intelligent beings are possible and will eventually
be run in large numbers. Debates about whether a sufficiently advanced
AI can be kept inside a box are closely related.
If the simulation hypothesis is false for *reasons of principle*, because minds cannot run on arbitrary environments or because a universe like ours cannot be feasibly computed, then any meaning would be arbitrary.

## 0.3 Who has taken it seriously

The idea has a respectable pedigree, which is not the same as being true, but
which does justify the effort of investigating it.

Among philosophers, Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper turned a piece of science-fiction
scenery into a formal argument with explicit premises [Bostrom 2003]; David
Chalmers has since devoted a full-length book to the metaphysics and
epistemology of virtual worlds [Chalmers 2022]. Among physicists, Silas Beane,
Zohreh Davoudi and Martin Savage asked what observable traces a universe
simulated on a lattice would leave [Beane, Davoudi & Savage 2012]; David
Kipping has tried to put a Bayesian number on the hypothesis [Kipping 2020];
Sabine Hossenfelder has argued, sharply, that the whole enterprise is
pseudoscience [Hossenfelder 2021]. Among technologists and public figures, Elon
Musk, George Hotz and Eric Weinstein have each said in public that they think
we are probably simulated, and Hotz has proposed that society should redirect
its efforts toward "getting out" [Yampolskiy 2022, §1].

Several of these people have attached numbers to their belief. Yampolskiy
collects them: Musk, better than 99.9999999 %; Bostrom, somewhere between 20 %
and 50 %; Neil deGrasse Tyson, 50 %; Hans Moravec, "almost certainly"; Kipping,
a little under 50 % [Yampolskiy 2022, §1].

## 0.4 Method: the investigation notebook

This book is written as an investigation notebook, and every chapter follows
the same template.

1. **Question under investigation.** One sentence. A chapter that cannot state
   its question in one sentence has not found it yet.
2. **Predictions.** What we should expect to observe if the answer is *yes*,
   and what if the answer is *no*. Where the two predictions coincide, the
   question is not empirical and the chapter says so.
3. **Evidence for**, with sources.
4. **Evidence against**, with sources.
5. **Provisional verdict.** A short judgement, written in the present tense,
   with a confidence level. It is provisional in the strict sense: a later
   chapter is free to overturn it, and the conclusions (chapter 7) keep a
   register of every reversal.
6. **Open threads.** What observation or argument would change the verdict.
7. **Sources.** Pointers into the bibliography, cited inline as
   `[Author Year]`.

Two rules of evidence apply throughout.

**Grade the source, not the speaker.** An argument from a Nobel laureate and
an argument from an anonymous forum post are graded by the same criteria: does
it make a prediction, is the prediction distinguishable from what standard
physics already predicts, and has anyone checked? Chapter 2 introduces an
explicit grading scheme for this purpose.

**A long list of weak arguments does not add up to a strong one.** Much of the
popular case for the simulation hypothesis takes the form "and here is another
thing that would make sense if we were simulated". Each such item, examined
alone, usually has an ordinary explanation. The book resists the temptation to
let twenty ordinary explanations feel like one extraordinary one.

## 0.5 What this book is not

It is not a survey of every route people have proposed for leaving or seeing
through the simulation. Yampolskiy explicitly sets aside meditation,
psychedelics, dreams, mysticism, near-death experiences and religion as escape
paths, and concentrates on the kinds of attack that appear in cybersecurity
research [Yampolskiy 2022, §1]. This book **does not** follows the same way since we don't care where the evidence come from.

It is not a theology. The hypothesis has a creator in it, and chapter 6 will
discuss what, if anything, that implies. But the book does not assume that the
creator is wise, good, attentive, or singular, in some way it hopes that the very **Being** is cunning and very full of itself.

And it is not a claim to a final answer. The last chapter states a credence and
a range.

## 0.6 How to read

The chapters are meant to be read in order. Chapter 1 fixes the definitions
that every later chapter uses; chapters 2 and 3 form a pair, prosecution and
defence; chapter 4 asks whether the case can ever be settled by observation.
Chapter 5 and its three sub-chapters follow Yampolskiy's paper on escaping the
simulation and can be skipped by readers who want only the question of *whether*
and not the question of *what then*. Chapters 6 and 7 close.

Citations appear inline in the form `[Author Year]` and resolve in
`99-bibliography.md`. Where a claim in the text is speculation rather than a
sourced statement, the text says so.
