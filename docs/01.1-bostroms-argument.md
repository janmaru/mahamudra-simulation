# 1.1 Bostrom's Argument

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper does not claim that we live in a simulation. It
claims that at least one of three propositions is true: civilisations almost
never reach a "posthuman" stage; posthuman civilisations almost never run
ancestor simulations; or we are almost certainly in one. This sub-chapter
reconstructs the argument step by step and isolates its load-bearing
assumptions.

## Question under investigation

Is the trilemma valid, and if so, which horn is the most credible?

## 1.1.1 The trilemma stated

Bostrom's conclusion is a disjunction. At least one of the following is true
[Bostrom 2003]:

1. The fraction of human-level civilisations that reach a posthuman stage is
   very close to zero.
2. The fraction of posthuman civilisations that are interested in running
   simulations of their evolutionary history, or variations of it, is very
   close to zero.
3. The fraction of all people with our kind of experiences that are living in
   a simulation is very close to one.

"Posthuman" here means a civilisation that has acquired most of the
technological capabilities consistent with physical law, including enough
computing power to run large numbers of detailed simulations of human minds.
"Ancestor simulation" means a simulation of the civilisation's own past,
detailed enough that the simulated people are conscious.

The third proposition is the one that concerns us, and Bostrom's route to it
is the observation that if (1) and (2) are both false — if a non-negligible
fraction of civilisations reach the posthuman stage, and a non-negligible
fraction of those run ancestor simulations — then simulated people vastly
outnumber unsimulated people, and a person who does not know which kind they
are should assign most of their credence to being simulated.

## 1.1.2 The assumptions

Three assumptions carry the argument. Each is stated explicitly in the paper
and each is a place where the argument can be attacked.

**Substrate independence.** A mind is a pattern of information processing, and
the same pattern implemented on silicon would be as conscious as it is on
neurons. Bostrom does not argue for this at length; he notes it is widely held
in philosophy of mind and proceeds. If it is false — if consciousness requires
biology, or requires something no computer can supply — then simulated
people are not people, the count of "observers with our kind of experiences"
excludes them, and the argument stops at the first step.

**Computing power.** A posthuman civilisation could afford to run many
ancestor simulations. Bostrom's estimates, drawn from the literature of the
time: a human brain performs on the order of 10¹⁶ to 10¹⁷ operations per
second; the entire history of human mental activity to date is on the order of
10³³ to 10³⁶ operations; a planet-sized computer built along lines already
proposed could perform around 10⁴² operations per second, and so could run the
whole of human history in a fraction of a second, or a great many copies of it
in a year [Bostrom 2003]. The figures are rough and the paper says so. What
matters is the ratio: even if the brain estimate is off by several orders of
magnitude, the planetary computer has enormous headroom.

Bostrom also notes that a simulation need not compute everything at full
resolution. The simulators can render the microscopic structure of a distant
star only if someone points a telescope at it, and can patch errors on the fly
before anyone notices. This lowers the cost estimate further and, as chapter 4
will note, is also the reason the hypothesis is so hard to test.

**The bland indifference principle.** If you know that a fraction *f* of all
observers with experiences indistinguishable from yours are simulated, and you
have no other information bearing on which kind you are, your credence that
you are simulated should be *f*. Bostrom calls this "bland" because it applies
only when the observers are subjectively indistinguishable, which is meant to
make it weaker and less controversial than the general indifference principles
used in other anthropic arguments.

## 1.1.3 The arithmetic

Let *f_P* be the fraction of human-level civilisations that become posthuman,
*N* the average number of ancestor simulations run by a posthuman civilisation
that is interested in running them, *f_I* the fraction of posthuman
civilisations that are so interested, and *H* the average number of people
who lived in a civilisation before it became posthuman. Then the fraction of
all human-type observers who are simulated is

    f_sim = (f_P · f_I · N · H) / (f_P · f_I · N · H + H)
          = (f_P · f_I · N) / (f_P · f_I · N + 1)

The *H* cancels. What remains is that *f_sim* is close to one whenever the
product *f_P · f_I · N* is large. Bostrom's point is that *N*, on the
computing-power assumption, is *enormous* — a single posthuman civilisation
could run millions or billions of ancestor simulations — so the product is
large unless *f_P* or *f_I* is tiny. That is exactly the trilemma: either
*f_P ≈ 0* (horn 1), or *f_I ≈ 0* (horn 2), or *f_sim ≈ 1* (horn 3).

The formula also shows why small changes in the inputs swing the result. If
*N* is 10⁶ and *f_P · f_I* is 10⁻³, *f_sim* is about 0.999. If *f_P · f_I* is
10⁻⁷, *f_sim* is about 0.09. The argument is not robust to the assumption that
posthuman civilisations are rare *and* mostly uninterested; it is robust only
to each of those being moderately true on its own.

## 1.1.4 Objections raised in the literature

**The indifference principle does too much work.** Brian Weatherson argued
that the bland indifference principle is not as bland as claimed, and that
accepting it commits one to conclusions in other cases that most people would
reject [Weatherson 2003]. Bostrom replied that the principle applies only to
cases of subjective indistinguishability and that Weatherson's counterexamples
do not meet that condition [Bostrom 2005]. The exchange did not settle the
matter, and the point recurs in chapter 2.2: every counting argument across
observers depends on a principle for distributing credence, and there is no
agreed principle.

**Selective scepticism.** Jonathan Birch pointed out that the argument's
empirical premises — about computing power, about the likely behaviour of
future civilisations — are derived from observations of *our* world. If the
conclusion is that our world is probably simulated, those observations may not
tell us anything about base reality, in which case the premises lose their
support [Birch 2013]. The argument, if it succeeds, undermines the evidence it
rests on. Bostrom's position is that this is a feature, not a bug: the
simulators would presumably have similar physics, and in any case the trilemma
still holds even if the credence assignments become less certain.

**The Bayesian reanalysis.** David Kipping reframed the question as a
comparison between two hypotheses — that we are in a physical universe, or in
a simulated one — and asked what posterior odds a reasonable agent should hold
[Kipping 2020]. His result is that the odds are close to even, with a slight
edge to the physical hypothesis, because any chain of nested simulations must
bottom out in a base reality, and that base reality is a physical universe
that is not itself simulated. The result flips toward simulation only if and
when a civilisation actually demonstrates the capacity to run conscious
simulations. Kipping's analysis is examined in chapter 2.2; here it matters
because it shows that a careful treatment of the same premises need not give
*f_sim ≈ 1*.

**The patch.** In 2011 Bostrom and Marcin Kulczycki published a correction to
the original argument [Bostrom & Kulczycki 2011]. The original had implicitly
assumed that the simulated civilisations themselves reach the posthuman stage
at the same rate as unsimulated ones, which need not hold: a simulator might
routinely shut down simulations before they get that far. The patched
argument reformulates the trilemma so that it no longer depends on this
assumption. Yampolskiy cites this episode as an illustration that "even the
argument about the Simulation Argument had a bug in it" [Yampolskiy 2022, §1],
which is fair, and also as an illustration that bugs get found, which is the
point he wants.

## 1.1.5 Which horn?

Bostrom declines to choose in the paper. Asked in 2007, he said: "My gut
feeling, and it's nothing more than that, is that there's a 20 percent chance
we're living in a computer simulation" [Tierney 2007]; his online FAQ carried
a similar figure — "less than 50 % ... something like in 20 %-region" — until
a 2025 revision removed the number in favour of "a substantial probability",
on the grounds that a figure conveys false precision [Bostrom FAQ]. The
considerations
for each horn are worth laying out, because the rest of this book is in effect
an attempt to decide among them.

**For horn 1 (almost no civilisation becomes posthuman).** Every technological
civilisation may destroy itself, or be destroyed, before reaching the required
level. This is the "Great Filter" family of arguments, and the silence of the
sky — no detected signals, no visible megastructures — is weak evidence for
it. It is also the horn with the least comfortable implications for us.

**For horn 2 (almost no posthuman civilisation runs ancestor simulations).**
Advanced civilisations may consider it unethical to create conscious beings
who suffer; may find it uninteresting after the first few; may have legal or
cultural prohibitions; may have needs and values so different from ours that
"simulating the ancestors" is not a thing they would think of. Bostrom notes
that this horn requires a *convergence*: nearly *all* posthuman civilisations
would have to refrain, since even one prolific simulator would dominate the
count.

**For horn 3 (we are almost certainly simulated).** This follows if the first
two horns are false. Its plausibility is therefore the plausibility that
posthuman civilisations are not vanishingly rare *and* not uniformly
abstinent. It is also the horn that, uniquely, might leave evidence in our
world, which is why it is the one an investigation can hope to make progress
on.

## Predictions

- **If horn 3 is true:** chapter 2.1 should find at least weak physical
  traces of resource constraints, and chapter 4 should be able to design a
  test.
- **If horn 1 or 2 is true:** the physical world should look exactly as an
  unsimulated world would, and the "traces" of chapter 2.1 should each have
  an ordinary explanation.

## Provisional verdict

The trilemma is valid: given its premises, at least one of the three
propositions must hold, and the 2011 patch closes the one formal gap that was
found. The premises themselves are the weak point. Substrate independence is
plausible but unproven; the computing estimates are defensible; the
indifference principle is contested and the counting it licenses is highly
sensitive to inputs we cannot measure. The argument therefore establishes
that the hypothesis is *not negligible*, and does not establish that it is
*likely*.

As to which horn: no verdict yet. Horn 3 is the only one that makes
predictions checkable from inside, so the investigation proceeds by trying to
check them.

Confidence in the validity claim: high. Confidence in any credence for horn 3:
low, pending chapters 2 through 4.

## Open threads

- Does the bland indifference principle survive scrutiny once one allows that
  simulated observers may not be *exactly* indistinguishable from unsimulated
  ones? If simulations are cheap, they may also be coarse.
- Is "ancestor simulation" the only kind that matters? If posthuman
  civilisations run many simulations that are *not* of their own past,
  observers like us may be rare among simulated observers, and the count
  changes. Chapter 1.2 opens the taxonomy.
- Birch's selective scepticism: if the conclusion undermines the premises,
  what is the stable credence, if any?
