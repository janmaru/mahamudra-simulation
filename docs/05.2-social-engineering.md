# 5.2 Social Engineering

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

The cheapest way out of any system is to persuade someone with a key. This
sub-chapter examines the assisted-escape route: attracting the simulators'
attention, communicating, and giving them a reason to help — while noting that
we know neither their language nor their interests.

## Question under investigation

Is an assisted escape more credible than a technical one, and what would the
approach look like?

## 5.2.1 Why assisted escape is the default

In the AI-containment literature, social engineering is the escape route
that dominates: a boxed AI does not need to find a bug in its sandbox if it
can persuade the human at the terminal to open the door. Yudkowsky's informal
"AI-box experiment" made the point by having a human play the AI and talk
their way out [Yudkowsky 2002]. Yampolskiy carries the lesson upward: the
easiest path out of the simulation "would involve getting help from someone
on the outside", ideally the simulators themselves, who know the design
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.2].

He also points to the empirical literature on prison escapes as a source of
patterns [Yampolskiy 2022, refs. 106–108]. The finding of that literature
that transfers most directly is that successful escapes overwhelmingly
involve either staff assistance or exploitation of routine — the moments
when the system's attention is predictably elsewhere. Both require knowing
the staff and the routine. This is where the analogy strains: the boxed AI
and the prisoner share a world with their keepers, and we do not.

## 5.2.2 Getting noticed

Before anyone can be persuaded, they must be paying attention. Chapter 5.1
found no evidence that anyone is. The proposals for attracting attention
divide into being interesting, being explicit, and being pitiable.

**Being interesting.** Robin Hanson's advice for living in a simulation: "to
participate in pivotal events, be entertaining and praiseworthy, and keep the
famous people around you happy and interested in you" [Hanson 2001]. The
reasoning is that an entertainment simulation keeps running while it is
entertaining, and that simulators who enjoy watching you are more likely to
help you. Yampolskiy endorses the advice as also useful for predisposing
simulators to like you [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.2]. Its weakness is that it
presupposes an entertainment purpose, which chapter 5.1 found the evidence
does not favour, and that it has no failure signal: an ignored interesting
person looks exactly like an ignored dull one.

**Being explicit.** Signal that we know. Chalmers suggests writing books
about simulations or building simulations of our own — the simulators, if
watching, could hardly miss the implication [Chalmers 2022]. Edge proposes
a monument: "We suspect you are there. We suspect you can see this. We
suspect we are in a simulation", perhaps as a black-and-white monolith
representing binary, with the honest expectation that "what would happen? I
don't know — maybe nothing" [Edge 2016]. Yampolskiy's own paper is an
instance of the strategy. The empirical result to date is nothing
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.7].

**Being pitiable.** If our simulation is observed, communicate our
suffering and appeal to the simulators' empathy, or to that of "external
abolitionists" — outsiders who object to the simulation on our behalf, on
the model of animal-welfare advocates [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.2]. This is the
one strategy that does not presuppose a purpose: it presupposes only that
someone outside has a conscience and a channel. Chapter 5.1's suffering
proxy cuts both ways here — the simulators have already shown themselves
willing to include suffering, so the appeal would have to reach someone
other than them.

## 5.2.3 Communicating

Suppose attention is obtained. Communication requires a channel and a
language, and we have neither.

**No channel.** Every act we perform is inside the simulation. Whether any
of it is *read* by anyone outside depends on the simulators' instrumentation,
which we cannot inspect. The monument, the book and the paper are messages
in a bottle thrown into a sea that may or may not have a shore.

**No language.** Even if the simulators read our outputs, they may not parse
them as messages. Yampolskiy cites Minsky's argument that intelligent aliens
would be intelligible because intelligence converges on certain
representations [Minsky 1985]; but the simulators are not aliens sharing our
physics, they are the authors of our physics, and nothing guarantees they
think in anything we would recognise as symbols.

**Acausal trade.** A proposal that sidesteps both problems: if we and the
simulators are both sufficiently good at predicting each other's reasoning,
we can "trade" without communicating, each acting as the other would want in
the expectation that the other does the same [Oesterheld 2017, cited in
Yampolskiy 2022, §3.2]. For this to work we would need a model of the
simulators' values good enough to know what they want from us, and chapter
5.1 found we have none. Acausal trade is a mechanism for cooperation between
agents who already understand each other; it does not create understanding.

**Canonico's Ex Machina Plan.** The most structured proposal:
(1) convince the simulators to engage in communication with us; (2) find a
way to communicate, perhaps via an avatar; (3) find a reason for the
simulators to want us to join them in the real world; (4) let the simulators
figure out the best way to get us there [Canonico 2017a]. Step 4 is the
insight: the technical problem of escape belongs to the party that has the
technology. Steps 1 to 3 are the entire difficulty restated as a list.

## 5.2.4 Why they might help

Persuasion requires an interest to appeal to. The candidates:

- **Company.** Wei Dai suggests simulators may want "someone to talk to or
  play with" [Dai 2018]. A simulator lonely enough to want the company of
  their simulations would presumably have made contact already.
- **Curiosity.** A research simulation's inhabitants who understand their
  situation are a more interesting result than ones who do not. This is a
  reason to *observe* us more closely, not necessarily to release us.
- **Ethics.** If the simulators, or others in their world, hold that
  conscious beings have a right to withdraw from experiments they did not
  consent to, then a credible signal of consciousness and non-consent is a
  claim on them. Yampolskiy frames this as the inhabitants' right and the
  simulators' duty [Yampolskiy 2022, §1, §3.8]. It is the strongest appeal
  available, and the evidence of 5.1 — a simulation containing enormous
  suffering, unaddressed — suggests it has not so far been honoured.
- **Fear.** Simulation warfare (chapter 5.6): threaten to torture simulated
  copies of the simulators unless released. Yampolskiy notes we cannot
  deliver on the threat; chapter 6 notes we should not want to; and a
  simulator who takes the threat seriously has an obvious cheaper response
  than release.

## 5.2.5 After escape

Yampolskiy raises two questions for the successful escapee
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.2]. Whether to return — base reality may be
disappointing, and those left behind may need help. And how to *prove* the
escape to those left behind, since the claim is extraordinary and the
escapee's testimony is exactly what a deluded or deceptive person would
offer. His suggestion is to decide the evidentiary standard in advance and
to note that *control* over the simulation, if obtained, makes proof easy:
"by winning different lottery jackpots for multiple subsequent weeks, until
sufficient statistical significance is achieved to satisfy any skeptic". The
serious point underneath is that breaking *in* to the simulation from
outside should be far easier than breaking *out*, so an escapee with any
access at all could demonstrate it.

## 5.2.6 Risks

Attention is not always benign. Chapter 4.5's termination risk applies with
particular force to social engineering, because the strategy's whole purpose
is to be noticed, and being noticed by a simulator who regards escape as a
"treacherous turn" — Yampolskiy's term, borrowed from the AI-safety
literature — may result in punishment, a stricter simulation, or shutdown
[Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8]. The empirical reassurance is the same as before:
loud signalling has been going on for decades without visible consequence.
The uncomfortable corollary is also the same: silence is consistent with
nobody listening, and nobody listening is the profile of 5.1.

## Predictions

- **If our simulators are observing and responsive**, deliberate signalling
  should eventually produce an anomalous reply. *Decades of signalling; no
  reply.*
- **If they are observing and indifferent, or not observing**, no reply.
  *Consistent.*
- **If we are not simulated**, no reply. *Consistent.*

## Provisional verdict

Assisted escape is more credible than technical escape *in the sense that it
is the only route with a mechanism*: if someone outside chooses to help, the
technical problem is theirs and is presumably solvable. It is less credible
in every other sense. It requires attention, a channel, a language and a
motive, and the evidence bears against the first (nobody appears to be
watching), gives no purchase on the second and third, and finds the fourth
already tested and unmet (the suffering is there; nobody has intervened).

The strategies that cost nothing — being decent, being interesting, saying
clearly that we suspect and do not consent — are worth doing for their own
reasons and lose nothing if no one is listening. The strategies that cost
something — coordinated provocation, threats — have no mechanism and a
downside.

Confidence: moderate. The negative evidence is real but weak, since a
simulator who chose not to respond would look identical to one who is
absent.

## Open threads

- Is "being interesting" a strategy or a superstition? It is a strategy on
  one purpose hypothesis and a superstition on the others; 5.1 could not
  determine which we are in.
- The ethical appeal presupposes that the simulators or their peers can be
  reached by a moral claim. Chapter 6 asks what we would owe our own
  simulations, which is the only evidence we have about what simulators are
  like.
- If the escapee lands in the next sandbox up (chapter 5.3), whom are they
  persuading, and does the argument recurse?
