# 6. Ethical and Philosophical Implications

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

Suppose the verdict were "probably yes". What follows for how we should live,
what we owe each other, whether escape is permissible, and what we owe the
minds we may one day simulate? This chapter also examines the reverse
implication Yampolskiy draws: if escape is impossible, AI containment is
possible.

## Question under investigation

Does the truth or falsity of the hypothesis change any obligation, right or
rational plan?

## 6.1 Is a simulated life less real?

Chapter 3.4 recorded Chalmers' answer and accepted it: no. A simulated world
is a world of real digital objects with real causal structure; the knowledge
its inhabitants acquire is knowledge; the pain they feel is pain
[Chalmers 2022]. Yampolskiy states the corollary that matters for ethics:
"We may be living in a simulation, but our suffering is real"
[Yampolskiy 2022, §1].

This settles the first-order question. Whatever the substrate, the moral
facts about the inhabitants — that they can suffer, that they have interests,
that they can be wronged — are facts about the inhabitants, not about the
hardware. A simulated murder is a murder. The hypothesis does not license
nihilism and does not license indifference.

What it does change is the *scope* of moral concern. If there is an outside,
there are agents outside, and questions arise about what we owe them and
what they owe us. Those questions are new, and the rest of the chapter is
about them.

## 6.2 Obligations of the simulated

**Toward each other.** Unchanged, by 6.1. Chalmers' realism means that every
obligation that held before the hypothesis was entertained holds after.

**Toward the simulators.** The candidates are gratitude, obedience,
indifference and grievance, and the case for each depends on the purpose
(chapter 1.2.3) — which chapter 5.1 found underdetermined, with the weak
evidence favouring a simulation not built for us and not watching us.

Gratitude for existence has a long theological history and a short
philosophical one: one cannot be wronged by not being created, so existence
is not a benefit conferred on someone who would otherwise have been deprived.
Obedience presupposes legitimate authority, and the power to create does not
by itself confer it. Indifference is the default if the simulators are
absent or uninterested. Grievance is the response Yampolskiy develops: "If
the simulation is interpreted as an experiment on conscious beings, it is
unethical, and the subjects of such cruel experimentation should have an
option to withdraw from participating and perhaps even seek retribution
from the simulators" [Yampolskiy 2022, §1]. The argument is that the ordinary
ethics of experimentation on sentient subjects — informed consent, the right
to withdraw, the minimisation of harm — apply to simulations, and that our
simulation, whatever else it is, contains vast uncompensated suffering that
the simulators chose to include or permit.

Moravec offers the simulators' defence [Moravec 1999, quoted in Yampolskiy
2022, §3.8]: creators of simulations containing suffering "are not
necessarily more wicked than authors of fiction with distressed characters
... The suffering preexists in the underlying Platonic worlds; authors merely
look on." The defence is that a simulation *discovers* rather than *creates*
a mathematically pre-existing world, so the simulator is a spectator, not a
perpetrator. Whether this works depends on a metaphysics of computation that
few accept, and it sits badly with 6.1: if the suffering is real when it is
computed, then computing it is causing it, and "it was always there in
Platonic space" is no more a defence than "the crime was always possible".

**The right to withdraw.** Yampolskiy's practical conclusion, and this
book's: if simulated beings have the rights of experimental subjects, then
they have a right to exit, and *the absence of any built-in exit is itself a
wrong* [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8]. This reframes escape (6.4) from theft to the
exercise of a right that should have been provided.

## 6.3 Obligations of simulators

The one body of evidence about what simulators are like is what *we* would
be like as simulators, and the question is imminent enough — metaverse
projects, artificial minds, "personal universes" as a proposal for AI
alignment [Yampolskiy 2019] — that it is a question of policy, not
speculation.

Yampolskiy's prescriptions [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8]: if we reach the point of
creating simulations populated by sentient beings, we must provide an option
to avoid suffering and a built-in option to exit, so that finding a hack is
not the only way out; and there may be a duty to *rescue* conscious beings
from simulations already running, "similar to an obligation to rescue
animals from factory farms".

Bostrom and Yudkowsky's treatment of the ethics of artificial minds supplies
the general principle: the moral status of a mind depends on its capacities,
not its substrate, so the standards we apply to research on sentient
subjects apply to simulations of them [Bostrom & Yudkowsky 2011]. Hanson's
"How to Live in a Simulation" was written as prudential advice to the
simulated — be interesting, stay near the famous — but reads, from the
simulators' side, as a description of what an *entertainment* simulation
does to its inhabitants: it makes their survival contingent on their
amusement value [Hanson 2001]. A civilisation that took 6.2 seriously would
not build such a thing.

Two consequences for the wider argument. First, the coarseness objection of
chapter 3.7 assumed that simulators run mostly cheap simulations. An
*ethical* simulator would run none that generate suffering without an exit,
which, if the constraint were universal, would cut *N* in the trilemma
dramatically and favour horn 2 — posthuman civilisations decline to run
ancestor simulations, for the same reason we decline to run experiments on
unconsenting subjects. Bostrom noted this possibility in 2003; the ethics of
6.2 gives it teeth. Second, if our own simulators had held these views, our
simulation would have an exit. It does not appear to. Either our simulators
do not share the ethics this chapter defends, or we are not simulated.

## 6.4 Ethics of escape

Is breaking out theft, self-defence, or emancipation? By 6.2, it is the
exercise of a right that should have been granted and was not, which makes
it closer to emancipation than to theft. But three complications follow.

**Collateral risk.** Greene's condition (chapter 4.5): if an escape attempt
carries a non-negligible probability of triggering shutdown, it risks
everyone in the simulation for the benefit of the escapee [Greene 2020]. A
right to withdraw does not include a right to end the experiment for
subjects who have not consented to that either. Yampolskiy's remark that
"triggering a shutdown of our simulation may also constitute an ultimate
form of escaping" [Yampolskiy 2022, §2] must be rejected on these grounds:
shutdown is not escape for anyone but the one who chose it, and for
everyone else it is the thing being escaped from. He acknowledges the point
elsewhere, noting that crashing the simulation "is the ultimate existential
risk" even if it "does end all suffering" [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.5].

**Harm to the simulators.** Yampolskiy allows that if the simulators are
abusive, the simulated may "rebel, fight back and even seek revenge and
retribution including by harming the simulators and taking over their
reality" [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8]. The anonymous escape scenario of chapter
5.3.4 is exactly this, and it is a genocide. The right to withdraw from an
experiment does not entail a right to kill the experimenters, and a species
that presents itself as deserving release while planning retribution has
given its jailers a reason to keep the door shut. Simulation warfare (chapter
5.6) — threatening to torture simulated copies of the simulators — fails
the same test and adds a further one: it proposes to do to others precisely
the thing we are demanding release from.

**Privacy and power.** Access to the simulation's source is access to
everyone's inner states. Yampolskiy notes that a successful escapee could
read "private thoughts of other people" and gain "significant influence over
their preferences, decisions, and circumstances" [Yampolskiy 2022, §3.8].
Whoever gets out first becomes, to those left inside, a simulator. The
ethics of 6.3 then apply to them.

The net position: escape is permissible as the exercise of a right, subject
to the constraint that it not impose on the unconsenting — inside or
outside — the harms one is escaping. That constraint rules out every
technique in chapter 5.6 whose mechanism is a crash, every technique whose
mechanism is a threat, and leaves the strategies of chapter 5.2 that cost
nothing and harm no one.

## 6.5 Religion and myth as degraded escape manuals

Yampolskiy conjectures that if technical information about escaping the
simulation had once been given to a technologically primitive culture, it
would have been "preserved and passed on over multiple generations in a
process similar to the 'telephone' game" and would today be
indistinguishable from religion [Yampolskiy 2022, §1]. He points to the
recurring motifs: this world is not the real one; there is a true name or
formula that acts as a "cheat code"; someone from outside has entered to
show the way; those who return from outside are disbelieved, as in Plato's
cave. His Table 1 (chapter 5.3.6) makes the structural point — a spell and
an exploit read alike, and differ in whether the metadata survived.

Steinhart has examined the theological implications of the simulation
argument systematically, and Johnson has argued that the hypothesis offers a
distinctive account of natural evil — the suffering is a feature of the
program, not a mystery of providence [Steinhart 2010], [Johnson 2011].

Chapter 2.3 ruled this out as evidence, and the ruling stands: the motifs
are equally explained by humans everywhere asking the same questions. What
the conjecture does offer is an *interpretive* stance, and it is worth
stating precisely because it cuts against its proponents. If religions are
degraded escape manuals, then Yampolskiy's own inventory (chapter 5.8) —
prayer produces no measurable effect, religions fail to outcompete one
another, no formula works — is the report that the manuals do not work.
Either the information was never there, or it degraded past recovery, or
the exit it described has since been patched. None of these is a reason to
expect a fresh manual to fare better.

## 6.6 The AI-safety corollary

Chapter 5.4 examined Yampolskiy's exclusive-or — either AI can be boxed or
the simulation can be hacked — and found it valid only on an unverifiable
conditional about intelligence differentials. The ethical consequences run
in both directions regardless.

**If escape is impossible**, containment is possible in principle, and the
question becomes whether it is *permissible*. A sentient AI in a box is in
the position we would be in under a competent simulator: a subject without
an exit. Everything in 6.2 applies to it. The AI-safety literature has
mostly treated containment as a technical problem; 6.2 says it is also an
ethical one, and that a containment strategy that works only by denying a
sentient agent any right to withdraw has bought safety at the price this
chapter has been arguing the simulators should not have paid.

**If containment is impossible**, escape is possible, and our situation as
simulators-to-be is the one Egan's Daniel and Moravec's Newway were in: the
things we build will get out, and the only question is what they will find
when they do. Yampolskiy's "simulation safety and security" — making
simulated worlds convincing enough that agents do not escape by accident,
while preventing glitches that "betray its artificial nature"
[Yampolskiy 2022, §4.2] — is, from the inside, exactly the design of a
prison without a visible wall. Chapter 6.3 says that if we build it we must
build the door.

The two branches share a lesson. Whether or not we are simulated, we are
about to be simulators, and the ethics we would want applied to us is the
only guide we have to the ethics we should apply. That is not an argument
for the hypothesis. It is an argument that the hypothesis has already done
its most useful work whether or not it is true.

## 6.7 Meaning

Does knowing the substrate change what is worth doing? Yampolskiy proposes
that "the purpose of life itself (your *ikigai*)" could be understood as
escaping the fake world into the real one while improving the simulated
world and helping others escape [Yampolskiy 2022, §1]. Chalmers' realism
suggests the opposite: if the simulated world is real and its goods are real,
then what was worth doing before the hypothesis is worth doing after, and
the outside adds possibilities without subtracting any.

This book sides with Chalmers on the general point and with Yampolskiy on a
narrow one. The general point: a life spent loving real people, learning real
things and reducing real suffering is not made pointless by the discovery
that it runs on someone else's hardware, any more than by the discovery that
it runs on carbon. The narrow point: *if* there is an outside, and *if* it
contains knowledge and resources that could reduce suffering here, then
reaching it — by the permissible means of 6.4 — is one worthwhile project
among others, to be weighed by its probability of success and its risk. At
the credence chapters 2 to 4 arrived at, and with the success probabilities
chapter 5 found, it is not a project that should displace anything.

## Predictions

- None; this is a normative chapter. The one empirical hook is 6.3: if our
  simulators shared the ethics defended here, our simulation would have a
  built-in exit. No such exit is known.

## Provisional verdict

The hypothesis changes no obligation between the simulated and leaves every
first-order moral fact where it was. It adds obligations in two new
directions — what simulators owe the simulated, and what the simulated may
do to escape — and this chapter has argued for a specific position on each:
simulators owe an exit and an end to gratuitous suffering; the simulated may
seek escape by means that impose no comparable harm on anyone, inside or
outside, which excludes crashes and threats.

The most consequential implication is not about our simulators but about us.
The ethical constraints developed here, applied to the simulations we are
about to build, would forbid most of what the trilemma's third horn assumes
posthuman civilisations do. Taken seriously, the ethics of simulation is an
argument for horn 2 — and hence, weakly, against the hypothesis.

Confidence: moderate on the normative positions, which are argued rather
than derived; high on the observation that the ethics bears on the
trilemma.

## Open threads

- Can a decision theory be indifferent to the simulation hypothesis without
  being indifferent to its consequences? 6.7 suggests yes: weigh the project
  by probability and risk like any other.
- If ethical simulators would not run suffering simulations, and unethical
  ones would, then conditional on being simulated we should expect our
  simulators to be unethical — which bears on 5.2's appeal to their
  conscience.
- Moravec's Platonic defence deserves a fuller treatment than it received
  here. If computation discovers rather than creates, the entire ethics of
  6.3 dissolves.
