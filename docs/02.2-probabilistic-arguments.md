# 2.2 Probabilistic Arguments

> **Status:** draft — full text, awaiting review.

## Abstract

Most of the intellectual weight behind the simulation hypothesis is not
physical but probabilistic: given plausible assumptions about future computing,
simulated observers should vastly outnumber real ones. This sub-chapter
examines that reasoning, the priors it depends on, and the attempts to make it
quantitative.

## Question under investigation

Do the probabilistic arguments deliver a credence materially above 50 %, and
are they robust to reasonable changes in their assumptions?

## 2.2.1 The counting argument

Stripped of Bostrom's careful framing (chapter 1.1), the argument is:

1. Most observers with experiences like ours are simulated.
2. You are an observer with experiences like ours.
3. Therefore you are probably simulated.

Premise 1 is where the trilemma does its work; if either of the first two
horns holds, premise 1 is false and the argument stops. This sub-chapter
assumes, for the sake of examination, that neither horn holds, and asks
whether the inference from 1 and 2 to 3 goes through.

It does not go through automatically. Moving from "most observers are X" to
"I am probably X" requires a rule for how to treat oneself as a sample, and
there are two such rules in the anthropic literature, which disagree.

**The self-sampling assumption (SSA).** Reason as if you were a random sample
from the set of all observers who actually exist. Under SSA, if simulated
observers actually outnumber unsimulated ones, you are probably simulated.
The argument goes through — *given that the simulated observers exist*.

**The self-indication assumption (SIA).** Reason as if you were a random
sample from the set of all *possible* observers, so that hypotheses on which
more observers exist are more likely to be true in proportion. Under SIA the
argument goes through even more strongly, because hypotheses with vast
numbers of simulated observers get a boost simply for containing more
observers.

Both rules have well-known unattractive consequences. SSA generates the
Doomsday argument (chapter 2.2.5) and depends on a choice of *reference class*
— which observers count as "like us" — that has no principled answer. SIA
generates the "presumptuous philosopher" problem: it lets one conclude, from
the armchair, that the universe is enormous simply because enormous universes
have more observers in them. Bostrom's bland indifference principle is an
attempt to use only the uncontroversial common ground: when observers are
*subjectively indistinguishable*, treat yourself as a random one of them.

The reference-class problem bites even here. "Subjectively indistinguishable"
does the work of defining the class, and chapter 1.2 noted that cheap
simulations may be coarse. If most simulated observers have experiences that
are *detectably* poorer than ours — fewer details, simpler physics, shorter
histories — then they are not in our class, and the count that matters is
the count of *high-fidelity* simulated observers, which may be far smaller.
Sean Carroll has pressed this point: a civilisation running many simulations
would presumably run mostly cheap ones, so a typical simulated observer should
expect to find itself in a low-resolution world, and we do not
[Carroll 2016]. Chapter 3 returns to this as an objection; here it is noted
as a reason the counting argument's premise 1 is harder to establish than it
looks.

## 2.2.2 Kipping's Bayesian analysis

David Kipping reformulated the question as a Bayesian model comparison
[Kipping 2020]. The setup: two hypotheses, that our universe is physical (not
simulated) and that it is simulated; a prior split evenly between them; and a
likelihood function that accounts for the structure of nested simulation.

The key structural point is that any simulation must be run by *something*,
and that something either is base reality or is itself a simulation run by
something else, and so on until base reality is reached. So under the
"simulated" hypothesis there is always exactly one physical universe plus some
number of simulated ones. Under the "physical" hypothesis there is one
physical universe and zero simulated ones. Kipping treats the number of
simulated universes as unknown and integrates over it.

His result: the posterior probability that we are simulated is slightly *less*
than one half. The intuition is that the "physical" hypothesis puts all its
weight on our being the one physical universe, while the "simulated"
hypothesis has to share its weight between that one physical universe and the
simulations — and since the number of simulations is unknown, the sharing
does not overwhelmingly favour being simulated. The result flips only if a
civilisation demonstrates the ability to run conscious simulations: at that
point the "no simulations exist" branch collapses and the posterior swings
toward being simulated.

Two features of the analysis deserve comment.

First, the even prior is doing real work. Kipping adopts it as a principle of
indifference between two hypotheses. But one could argue that "the universe
contains nested conscious simulations" is a far more specific and demanding
claim than "it does not", and deserves a lower prior; or, from the other
side, that if simulations are possible at all they are cheap and therefore
likely, and the hypothesis deserves a higher one. The result is sensitive to
this choice.

Second, the analysis confirms, from a different direction, the point made in
chapter 1.1.3: the arithmetic is only overwhelming if one already assumes that
simulations are numerous. Bostrom's *f_sim ≈ 1* and Kipping's *< 0.5* are
both correct calculations from different assumptions about what we know.

**Grade.** C. A careful argument that delivers a credence near even.

## 2.2.3 Nested simulations and the resource budget

Chapter 1.2.2 introduced nesting: a simulation good enough to contain
civilisations that build computers may contain simulations of its own. The
counting argument is sometimes strengthened by appeal to this — "and each of
those simulations contains more, so the ratio is even larger". The resource
argument shows why that strengthening fails.

Base reality has some finite computational budget *C*. Everything computed at
level 1 costs base-reality resources, so the total computation at level 1 is
at most *C*. Everything computed at level 2 is computed *by* level-1
computers, which are themselves computed at level 1, so the total at level 2
is at most the fraction of *C* that level-1 civilisations devote to running
level-2 simulations — and so on. The sum over all levels is bounded by *C*;
nesting does not create compute, it partitions it. Deeper levels are cheaper
only if they are coarser, which returns us to the reference-class problem.

Yampolskiy notes the practical consequence for escape: a successful breakout
from a deep level might land one in the next sandbox up, not in base reality,
and the two might be indistinguishable from inside [Yampolskiy 2022, §2]. The
consequence for counting is that the expected number of *high-fidelity*
observers at all simulated levels combined is bounded by what base reality
can afford, and the multiplier that makes *f_sim* large has to come from base
reality's budget being enormous relative to the cost of a mind. That is
Bostrom's computing-power premise again, not a new argument.

## 2.2.4 The self-fulfilling clause

Yampolskiy observes that we can *change* the probability that we are in a
simulation by committing to run many ancestor simulations ourselves: if we
will run them, then civilisations like ours run them, so the second horn of
the trilemma is false for us, and the count moves toward horn 3
[Yampolskiy 2022, §1, refs. 66–67].

The observation is correct as a point about *evidence* and incorrect as a
point about *causation*, and the two are easy to confuse.

As evidence: our own intentions are data about what civilisations like ours
do. If we find ourselves intending to run ancestor simulations, that is weak
evidence that other civilisations at our stage also intend to, which raises
our estimate of *f_I* and hence of *f_sim*. This is legitimate; it is the
same reasoning by which finding life on Earth is weak evidence that life is
common.

As causation: nothing we decide now can change whether we are *already*
simulated. The simulation, if it exists, is running; our commitment cannot
reach back and start it. The phrasing "we can increase our probability of
being in a simulation" is true only in the sense that we can increase our
*credence*, by learning something about ourselves.

The distinction matters because the clause is sometimes offered as a *reason*
to run simulations — as if doing so would improve our own prospects. It would
not. It would only improve our estimate of what those prospects already are.

**Grade.** C, and a small update. Our intentions are one data point about one
civilisation.

## 2.2.5 The Doomsday and Fermi connections

Two neighbouring arguments bear on the first horn of the trilemma and hence,
indirectly, on the third.

**The Great Filter.** The Fermi paradox — the universe appears old and large
enough to have produced many civilisations, yet we detect none — suggests that
something prevents civilisations from becoming visible on a cosmic scale.
Robin Hanson called this the Great Filter [Hanson 1998]. If the filter lies
*ahead* of us — if civilisations at our stage usually fail to become
posthuman — then horn 1 of the trilemma is true, *f_P ≈ 0*, and the counting
argument for simulation collapses. The silence of the sky is therefore weak
evidence *against* the simulation hypothesis, by way of being weak evidence
for horn 1.

There is a counter-move: perhaps the sky is silent *because* we are simulated,
and rendering other civilisations is expensive, so the simulators left them
out. This is speculation of the "it would explain X" form rejected in chapter
2.3 — it makes no prediction about *how* silent the sky should be, or whether
we should find microbial life, or anything else that could be checked — and
it is recorded here only for completeness.

**The Doomsday argument.** Under SSA, treating yourself as a random human
among all humans who will ever live implies that the total number is probably
not vastly larger than the number so far, which implies that humanity's
future is probably short. This is the same inference rule the counting
argument uses, applied to a different reference class, and it delivers a
conclusion most people reject. Either the rule is wrong, in which case the
counting argument is in trouble, or the rule is right and we should accept
both conclusions. The literature has not resolved this, and the simulation
argument inherits the unresolved status.

## 2.2.6 Sensitivity analysis

The table shows how the credence in horn 3 moves as the key inputs vary,
using the formula of chapter 1.1.3. *N* is the number of ancestor simulations
per interested posthuman civilisation; *f_P · f_I* is the product of the
fractions that reach the posthuman stage and choose to simulate.

| *N* | *f_P · f_I* | *f_P · f_I · N* | *f_sim* |
|-----|-------------|-----------------|---------|
| 10⁶ | 10⁻¹ | 10⁵ | ≈ 0.99999 |
| 10⁶ | 10⁻³ | 10³ | ≈ 0.999 |
| 10⁶ | 10⁻⁶ | 1 | 0.5 |
| 10⁶ | 10⁻⁹ | 10⁻³ | ≈ 0.001 |
| 10³ | 10⁻³ | 1 | 0.5 |
| 10⁹ | 10⁻⁶ | 10³ | ≈ 0.999 |

The pattern is that *f_sim* is a step function of the product *f_P · f_I · N*:
close to 1 when the product is much greater than 1, close to 0 when it is much
less, and passing through 0.5 at exactly 1. Every input spans many orders of
magnitude in the literature. *N* depends on the computing-power premise and
on how many simulations an interested civilisation would run — thousands?
billions? *f_P* depends on the Great Filter. *f_I* depends on the ethics and
interests of beings we know nothing about.

The honest summary is that the product is *unknown to within many orders of
magnitude*, that 1 is comfortably inside the range of ignorance, and that a
credence anywhere from a few percent to a few tens of percent is defensible.
A credence above 90 % requires being confident that the product is above 10,
and no input supports that confidence.

## Predictions

- None directly observable. Probabilistic arguments of this kind make
  predictions only about what we should *believe*, not about what we should
  *see*. The exception is the self-fulfilling clause, which predicts that our
  own trajectory is informative — and chapter 4 notes that actually running a
  conscious simulation would be the one event that moves Kipping's posterior
  decisively.

## Evidence for

- The counting argument is valid *given* its premises, and its premises are
  not known to be false.
- Kipping's analysis, with an even prior, lands near one half — far above
  the credence anyone would have assigned before the argument was made.
- Our own intentions toward simulation are weak evidence about *f_I*.

## Evidence against

- The inference from "most observers are simulated" to "I am probably
  simulated" requires an anthropic sampling rule, and every such rule has
  consequences its proponents reject elsewhere.
- The reference class is underdetermined, and the coarseness point suggests
  that high-fidelity simulated observers may be rare even if simulated
  observers are common.
- The product *f_P · f_I · N* is unknown across many orders of magnitude and
  1 is inside the range, so the calculated credence is dominated by the
  choice of inputs rather than by any measurement.
- Nesting does not multiply compute; it partitions it.
- The Great Filter, if ahead of us, defeats the argument at horn 1.

## Provisional verdict

The probabilistic arguments do **not** deliver a credence materially above
50 %, and they are **not** robust to reasonable changes in their assumptions.
What they do deliver is a credence that is *not negligible*: a reasonable
agent, having followed the arguments, should hold something in the range of
a few percent to a few tens of percent, and should hold it with wide error
bars. Chalmers' stated floor of about 25 % and Kipping's "a little under one
half" are both inside this range; Musk's "one in billions against base
reality" is not, and is not supported by any calculation in the literature.

Combined with chapter 2.1, the case for the hypothesis at the end of the
prosecution is: no direct physical evidence; physical hints that add nothing
once ordinary physics is accounted for; and a probabilistic argument that
establishes possibility at a level worth taking seriously, but not likelihood.

Confidence: high on the sensitivity conclusion, which is arithmetic; moderate
on the range of defensible credences, which depends on judgements about the
anthropic sampling rules that the literature has not settled.

## Open threads

- Can an anthropic argument ever be checked against data? The only proposed
  check is to run a conscious simulation ourselves, which would move the
  posterior but raises the ethical issues of chapter 6.
- The coarseness objection (Carroll) needs a full treatment. If it holds, it
  is the strongest single argument against the hypothesis, because it
  converts the counting argument's own logic into evidence the other way.
  Chapter 3.
- The Doomsday parallel: if the counting argument and the Doomsday argument
  share a rule, a reader's attitude to one should constrain their attitude to
  the other. This book has not yet taken a position on the rule.
